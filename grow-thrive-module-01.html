The Mind, Mental Noise & Why Struggle Backfires

~18 minutes Read · try · reflect · self-check Private to you

By the end of this module you can

  • Explain why the mind naturally generates "mental noise" — and why that's normal.
  • Spot the trap of pushing feelings away (psychologists call it experiential avoidance) — and why it backfires.
  • Articulate the ACT alternative in one line: workability over control.
  • Spot your own "struggle switch" and what flipping it on costs you.

01 · The noisy mind

Your mind won't shut up —
and that's normal

Here's a relief and a challenge in one sentence: there is nothing wrong with you. The human mind evolved over hundreds of thousands of years to keep you alive — and the way it does that is by never switching off. It scans for threats, replays mistakes so you don't repeat them, compares you to others, and runs worst-case scenarios on a loop. In a world of sabre-tooth tigers, a mind that shouted "danger!" ten times too often kept you alive. Today, that same machinery fires before a penalty, an exam, or when you open your phone.

Athletes call it mental noise: the chatter, the criticism, the catastrophising, the "you're going to mess this up" that arrives uninvited at the worst possible moment. It's easy to assume the top performers have somehow switched it off. They haven't. The noise is not the problem.

"The problem was never the noise. It's the struggle with it."

— The core idea of Module 01

So if you can't delete the noise — and you can't — the real question becomes: what do you do when it shows up? That single shift, from trying to control what's in your head to changing your relationship with it, is the doorway to everything else in THRIVE.

02 · A 30-second experiment

Try not to think about it

Reading about suppression won't convince you. Feeling it will. This is the classic thought-suppression task — do it properly and honestly, and it'll teach you more than any lecture.

Experiential exercise

The white-bear task

For the next 30 seconds, your one job is simple: do not think about a white bear. Not its fur, not its eyes, not the word. Push it out of your mind completely. Start the timer and try as hard as you can.

Do NOT think about a white bear
0:30

Push the thought away. Try hard. We'll talk on the other side.

So — how did that go? If you're like almost everyone, the harder you pushed, the more the bear showed up. That's the point. Trying not to think about something is itself a reminder to think about it. Now swap "white bear" for "don't be nervous," "don't choke," "don't think about the score." The mind works the same way. Suppression doesn't just fail — it often amplifies.

Psychologists have run this experiment for decades (Wegner's work on "ironic processes"). The finding is remarkably consistent: deliberately suppressing a thought tends to make it more frequent and more sticky — especially under stress, which is exactly when athletes reach for it.

03 · The struggle switch

The one move that
turns pain into suffering

Russ Harris uses a simple, powerful image: imagine you have a "struggle switch" at the back of your mind. When it's ON, any uncomfortable feeling that shows up becomes a problem to be fought. Anxiety shows up → you get anxious about being anxious → then frustrated that you're anxious about being anxious. The original feeling snowballs. You now have a whole layer of secondary suffering piled on top.

When the switch is OFF, the same anxiety can show up — but you're not at war with it. It rises, it's unpleasant, and… it's allowed to be there. No snowball. The feeling runs its course. You still feel it; you just don't add the second arrow.

  • Switch ON: pain + struggle = suffering that grows and pulls your attention off the job.
  • Switch OFF: pain, felt cleanly, that you can carry while you do what matters.

Notice what this means: the goal was never to feel calm. Elite performers feel nerves, doubt and pressure just like you — sometimes more. The difference is they've learned to leave the struggle switch off and act anyway. That's emotional flexibility, not emotional suppression — and it's the exact opposite of "harden up."

"You can't always control the wave. You can learn to stop fighting the water."

— On the struggle switch

04 · The ACT alternative

Ask "does it work?"
not "is it true?"

If controlling the noise is a losing game, what replaces it? This is where ACT quietly breaks with almost everything you've been taught about thinking. The answer is a single, practical yardstick: workability.

Start with how your mind normally runs: like a courtroom. All day it hands down verdicts — right or wrong, true or false, good or bad, fair or unfair — and it recruits you as the lawyer: gather the evidence, argue the case, win. Here's the problem: the courtroom never adjourns. You can beat "I'm not good enough" at ten o'clock tonight and be back in the dock by seven tomorrow, facing the same charge. ACT's move is simple and radical: walk out of the courtroom and into the workshop. In a workshop, nothing is right or wrong. Things are useful or not useful for the job in hand. And the only question that ever matters in a workshop is:

"Right here, right now — is what I'm doing moving me towards the person and player I want to be, or away?"

— The workability question

Underneath this sits ACT's most surprising idea, and it's worth slowing down for: thinking is a behaviour. Something you do — like passing, sprinting or tackling — just performed privately, inside your head. That one idea changes the game twice over.

First: behaviours aren't true or false. Nobody asks whether a pass is "true" — you ask whether it was on target, for this moment, for this purpose. The same goes for a thought. "I might get dropped" isn't a fact to disprove or a verdict to accept — it's a mental action your brain just performed. The only real question is whether rehearsing it, right now, helps you play. Second: behaviours can be trained. You don't choose the first thought that shows up — your mind serves that automatically, like a reflex. But everything that happens next — replay it, argue with it, obey it, or notice it and return to the job — is behaviour too. And behaviour is exactly the thing training changes.

Now watch what this does to black-and-white thinking. "The ref is terrible" might be completely true — and rehearsing it at half-time is still unworkable, because it points every watt of your attention at something you can't control. "I'm the best player on this pitch" might feel great — and still be unworkable if it stops you listening to the game plan. Flip it: a blunt, uncomfortable thought can be genuinely workable ("my touch is off today — simplify"), and a shiny positive one can be a trap. Truth and usefulness are different axes. The courtroom only ever asks the first question. Performers live and die on the second.

That's the shift in practice. Fighting your nerves before a game might feel like the responsible thing to do — but if it drains your focus and tightens your body, it isn't working. Avoiding the session because you feel flat buys an hour of relief — and moves you away from the athlete you want to be. Workability cuts through the endless debate about whether a thought is right and asks the only question that changes anything: where is this taking me?

Everything from here — the three ACE moves — is built to help you answer that question well: Acknowledge the noise, Connect to the present, and Engage with what matters. Module 01's job was just to show you why the old strategy of control keeps failing. That's the ground the whole programme stands on.

Apply in Sport

Name a recent moment when fighting your nerves made things worse — a rushed warm-up, a tight kick, a snappy word to a teammate. Notice: the struggle, not the nerves, did the damage.

Apply in Life

Where else do you flip the switch? Replaying an awkward text? Forcing yourself to sleep? Avoiding an assignment because it feels heavy? Same move, same cost — off the field too.

05 · Reflect

Make it yours

This is where the idea lands. Be specific and be honest — no one sees this but you. Answer these in your notebook — one honest line each is plenty.

In your notebook

Name a recent moment when fighting your nerves (or a thought) made things worse.

What was the situation, what did you try to push away, and what did it cost you?

In your notebook

Where else — outside sport — do you flip the struggle switch on?

Log one non-sport example. Naming it is where the transfer habit starts.

In your notebook

In your own words — what's the cost of the struggle switch being on?

This is your retrieval question. Say it how you'd explain it to a younger teammate.

Lock it in

Talk it over

Take one of these to a mate, your team, or your coach this week. Saying it out loud is where it gets real.

  • Think of the best competitor you know. Do they have a quieter mind than you — or a different relationship with the noise? What do you actually know about what goes on in their head?
  • Where in your sport do people treat true and useful as the same thing? (“He’s faster than me” might be true — when is it useful to rehearse it?)
  • What’s one thing your sport’s culture tells you to do with feelings that this module says backfires?

One card to carry: the noise is normal, the struggle is the cost, and the question that matters is "is this working?" — not "is this true?" That's the whole foundation. From here, THRIVE teaches the three moves that let you act well with the noise: Acknowledge, Connect, Engage. First up — Acknowledge: making room for what shows up.
