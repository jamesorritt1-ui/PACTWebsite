Read this first

One routine.
Every moment.

You’ve probably met ACE already. This is the page where it stops being three separate skills and becomes your routine.

ACE stands for Acknowledge, Connect, Engage. Acknowledge what’s showing up inside you. Connect with your body and the place you’re in. Engage with the job in front of you. Three moves, always in that order.

What makes it so useful is that it stretches. The same three moves can take one breath on a start line or ten minutes in a changing room. Short gaps get a short ACE. Long build ups get a long one. Because the moves never change, whatever length you need on the day, your body and brain already know the shape.

Why one routine instead of ten tools?

Under pressure, your brain doesn’t go searching through a library. It grabs whatever is most practised. Ten tools you half know will mostly stay on the shelf. One routine you’ve run hundreds of times is there when you need it.

So think of ACE as the frame, and the rest of your Skills Journal as what goes inside it. The skills in Open give your Acknowledge its words. The skills in Aware and the Practice Room give your Connect its moves. Your values, cue words and imagery from Engaged and Using Imagery Well give your Engage its direction.

An anchor, not an off switch. ACE is not a way to make nerves, doubt or anger disappear. Sometimes they do shrink while you run it. Enjoy that, but don’t chase it. The job of ACE is to give your attention somewhere to come back to, so you can do the next thing well with the noise still there. If you judge ACE by whether you feel calm, you’ll decide it has failed on exactly the days you need it most.

Does it actually work?

Routines before performance have solid research behind them. A review that pooled studies across fifteen sports found athletes performed better once they had learned a routine, including under pressure. That held for beginners and elite athletes, younger and older athletes, and for simple and detailed routines alike.

Mindfulness and acceptance approaches, the family ACE comes from, also show promising effects on performance in sport, though the researchers are honest that we still need more high quality trials. The fair summary: a practised routine helps, practising it matters more than the exact ingredients, and ACE gives you one built to work with nerves rather than against them.

01

The Three Moves

What each move is for, how it sounds, and the traps that turn it into something else.

One job each

Each move has one job. If you know the job, you can make the move fit any sport, any length of time and any kind of day.

A

Acknowledge

Notice what’s here and name it, in a few words, without arguing.

Why we do it

You can’t steer around something you haven’t noticed. When thoughts and feelings go unnamed, they run things from backstage: tight shoulders, rushed decisions, playing it safe, going quiet. Naming them turns them from orders into information.

There’s good brain research behind this. Putting a feeling into words takes a little heat out of the brain’s alarm system. And trying hard not to think about something tends to make it come back louder. This is also the step that stops ACE becoming another way of running from the hard stuff. Skip it and you haven’t got an anchor, you’ve got another escape route.

What it sounds like

  • “There’s the ‘don’t stuff this up’ thought.”
  • “Heart’s going. Hands are sweaty.”
  • “Frustrated about that call. Fair enough.”
  • “Noticing the urge to hide.”
  • “Hello, old story.”

Watch out for

  • Arguing with it. “I’m not nervous, I’m fine.”
  • Lecturing yourself about it.
  • Turning the name into a judgement. “I’m so weak for feeling this.”
  • Taking ten seconds when you’ve only got two.

Smallest version

One word. “Nerves.” “Story.” “Angry.” Or just a nod to yourself that says there it is.

C

Connect

Land in your body and in the place you’re actually in.

Why we do it

Your thoughts can be in last week’s game or next week’s selection. Your body is always right here, right now. Coming back to it gives you something steady to stand on while the noise carries on. It also hands you back the one thing you can always control under pressure: what your arms and legs actually do.

A slow breath out that’s longer than the breath in can take some of the edge off a revving body. Treat that as a bonus. The main point is to land, not to force calm.

What it looks like

  • Press your feet into the ground, or your toes into your boots.
  • Feel your grip on the stick, bat, ball, racquet or oar.
  • One slow breath out, longer than the breath in.
  • Eyes on one fixed, real thing: the seam of the ball, a line on the court, the top of the posts.
  • Long version: five things you can see, three you can hear.

Watch out for

  • Using it to force calm. “Breathe. Breathe. Why isn’t this working?”
  • Making it so big your teammates think you’re having a moment. Nobody needs to see it.
  • Closing your eyes in a fast game.
  • If focusing on your breath makes you feel worse, skip it. Feet, hands and eyes work just as well.

Smallest version

One breath out, or press your heels down. Two seconds.

E

Engage

Point your attention at the next job, with your values leading, and commit.

Why we do it

ACE always finishes on the task, because that’s the whole point of it. Attention aimed outward, at a target, a space or the effect you want, tends to keep movement smooth. A list of body parts, or a “don’t miss”, tends to make it tight.

Picking the next job, rather than the scoreboard or the result, keeps you in the part of the game you can actually affect. In the long version, your values come in too: who you want to be out there today, whatever the score.

What it sounds like

  • “Next job.”
  • “Through the ball.” “Hit the space.”
  • “Strong and long.” “Talk early.”
  • A picture, like Lowell Bailey’s bow and arrow.
  • Long version: a value word for the day. “Brave.” “Relentless.” “Connected.”

Watch out for

  • An outcome instead of an action. “Win this point.”
  • A technique lecture. “Elbow up, wrist firm, follow through, weight forward.”
  • “Don’t” cues. Your body needs a direction, not a ban.
  • Engaging with the scoreboard instead of the ball.

Smallest version

One word or one picture, then go.

Why the order matters
  1. A before C. If you skip Acknowledge and go straight to breathing or focusing, you’re really trying to push the feeling away. That usually makes it louder. Noticing it first takes away a lot of its pull.
  2. C before E. It’s hard to aim your attention from a body that’s spinning. Landing first gives Engage a steady place to launch from.
  3. Always finish on E. An ACE that ends on Acknowledge leaves you sitting with the feeling. An ACE that ends on Engage leaves you doing the next thing.

And if the noise grabs you again a few seconds later? Run it again. Every time you notice you’ve drifted and come back, that’s a rep, not a failure.

One skill, four tempos

Think about how you learned any skill at training. You did it slowly first, then at game speed, then under pressure. ACE works the same way. You train the long version so the short version works. The short one is just the long one with the volume turned down.

What changes as you turn the dial
  1. How much you name. One word at one breath. A full check in of thoughts, feelings, body and urges at ten minutes.
  2. How wide you go. A short ACE stays narrow: your feet, the ball. A long ACE widens out to the whole room, then narrows back down to the job.
  3. How much you plan. A short ACE picks the next job. A long ACE rehearses your first few jobs and what you’ll do after your first mistake.

What never changes: the order, and finishing on E.

03

The 40 Second ACE

For the gaps between plays: after a mistake, between points, during a stoppage. Forty seconds is plenty.

What it is

Most sports are full of short gaps. Ten seconds here, thirty seconds there. Those gaps matter more than they look, because they’re where the last play either gets left behind or gets carried into the next one. The 40 second ACE is a reset that fits into almost any of them.

0 to 10 s
A · Acknowledge

Let it land, then name it. React if you need to: a breath, a shake of the head. Then a few words. “That’s the miss.” “Angry. Fair enough.”

10 to 20 s
C · Connect

Feet into the ground. One long breath out. Eyes on something fixed and real. Something with your hands: regrip, adjust your gloves, straighten your strings.

20 to 35 s
E · Engage

Look up and out. What’s happening? Where’s the space? What’s the next job? Say your cue: “Next job.” “Talk early.” “Hit the target.”

35 to 40 s
Go

Commit. Shoulders back, eyes on the job. If the noise comes back as you go, let it come along.

Got less time? Squash it. Fifteen seconds: name it in one word, one breath out, cue, go. Five seconds: one breath that says there it is, then your cue. Lose the time, keep the order.

Worked example · after a mistake · rugby

Tama is 17 and plays on the wing for his club’s under 18s. Tama is made up, but this moment isn’t. He has just shot out of the defensive line, missed the tackle, and watched them score. He has about forty seconds behind the posts while the conversion is lined up.

A“That’s on me. Here’s the ‘you’ve cost us’ story.” Heavy chest. CHands on knees for one breath out, then stands tall. Presses his studs into the grass. Eyes on the halfway line. E“Next job: chase the kick off, stay connected to my inside man.” Cue: “Square and patient.”

He still feels gutted. He also makes his next tackle, because his attention is on it and not on the replay.

Worked example · between points · tennis

Aroha is 19 and plays for her university. She’s made up too. She’s just been broken to go down 4 to 5, and she has the twenty five seconds between points before she receives.

A“Nerves. And the ‘you always fold here’ thought.” CTurns her back to the net, walks to the back fence, straightens her strings, one slow breath out. E“Return deep, through the middle. Move first.” Bounces on her toes. Go.

Worked example · the walk back · cricket

Sam is 21, bowls seam for his club’s premier side, and is also made up. He’s just been hit for four. The walk back to his mark takes about twenty five seconds.

A“Annoyed. Urge to bowl it quicker and shorter.” CRubs the ball on his trousers, feels the seam, one long breath out as he turns at his mark. EEyes on the top of off stump. Cue: “Hit the hard length.” Runs in.

Notice he named the urge. Urges are often the most useful thing to catch, because they’re what turns one bad ball into a bad over.

04

The 10 Minute ACE

For the changing room, the bus, the call room or the night before. The long version that loads the short ones.

What it is

The changing room is one of the noisiest places in sport. Music, banter, nerves, the coach’s talk, studs on concrete, and your own head running through everything that could go wrong. Most athletes either try to block it all out or get swept along by it. The 10 minute ACE is a third option: an unhurried run through all three moves, so you walk out landed and pointed at your first job.

Why bother with a long one?

Two reasons. First, it’s a proper landing before a big moment, at the time when the build up is loudest. Second, it’s practice. Every time you run the long version, you’re rehearsing the exact moves your 40 second and one breath versions are made of. That’s what Sadie Bjornsen is doing the night before her race.

0:00 to 2:30
ACheck in

Sit somewhere you can stay for a few minutes: your spot on the bench, a bus seat, headphones on or off. Go through the four things that show up and name each one.

Thoughts: what’s your mind saying about today? Feelings: as precisely as you can. Body: a slow scan, head to toe. Urges: to talk, to hide, to check your phone, to go to the toilet again.

Make a little room for the ones you don’t like. You’re not trying to change any of it yet.

2:30 to 5:00
CLand

Feet flat. Feel the bench under you. A few slow breaths with long breaths out. If breathing work suits you, try two short breaths in through the nose and one long sigh out.

Press, stretch or roll something. Then widen: five things you can see, three you can hear, what you can feel on your skin. Notice the room as it is, noisy and all.

5:00 to 8:30
EPoint

Three questions. Who do I want to be out there today? One or two value words. What’s my first job? Your first touch, tackle, serve or fifty metres. What will I do after my first mistake? Because there will be one.

Then run your first two or three anchor moments in your head, at real speed, with the noise written in and your ACE inside each one. Using Imagery Well shows you how.

8:30 to 10:00
GoLoad the short ones

Run your 40 second ACE once, then your one breath ACE three times. Stand up. Rejoin the team, the warm up or the call room.

You’re not trying to hold on to how you feel right now. You’ve just loaded the routine you’ll use when you need it.

Fitting it into a real changing room
  1. You don’t need silence. Headphones, a towel over your head, or just looking at your boots all work. Nobody needs to know what you’re doing.
  2. Split it around the team. Do A and C before the team talk, and E after it. Team rituals like a huddle, a haka or the captain’s last word make great Engage moments.
  3. Short on time? Run it at five minutes: one minute each for A and C, two for E, one to load the short versions.
  4. Individual sport? The call room, the car park, the start hut or the bike on the trainer all work. Same shape.
  5. The night before? Run it in bed, the way Sadie Bjornsen does, then let it go and sleep. Keep it under ten minutes so it doesn’t turn into overthinking.
Worked example · a final · netball

Mere is 18 and plays goal attack. She’s made up, and so is the final, but the changing room will feel familiar. She has about ten minutes before warm up.

ATwo and a half minutes. Thoughts: “Their keeper is huge.” “Don’t let the team down.” Feelings: excited and scared at the same time. Body: butterflies, legs a bit jelly. Urges: another toilet trip, check her phone. “OK. That’s all here. Makes sense. It’s a final.” CTwo and a half minutes. Feet flat on the concrete, back against the wall. Three slow breaths out. Five things she can see: her bag, a teammate retaping, the whiteboard, the light, her shoes. Three sounds: the music, a whistle outside, someone laughing. EThree and a half minutes. Value: brave. First job: drive hard off the first centre and present early. After her first mistake: “Next ball,” hands up, call for it. Then she runs the first centre pass, a shot under pressure and a turnover against her in her head, with the “don’t let them down” thought showing up in each one. GoA minute and a half. Her 40 second ACE for after a missed shot, once. Three one breath ACEs: breath out, “up and soft.” Stands up for the huddle.

She walks out nervous. She also walks out with a plan for the nerves.

05

Build Your Routine

Hook each speed to a moment that already happens, and pull it all into one card.

Hook it to something that already happens

The best routine in the world is useless if you forget to run it. Habits stick when they’re obvious and easy, so tie each speed of ACE to a moment that already happens every time: retying your laces, walking back to your mark, adjusting your cap, chalking up, drying your hands, the half time whistle. The moment becomes the reminder.

  1. Choose a trigger for each speed. Something that happens every game, whether you’re playing well or badly.
  2. Keep the words short and yours. If you wouldn’t say it out loud to a teammate, don’t say it to yourself.
  3. Make the body part invisible. If someone filmed you, they shouldn’t be able to spot it.
  4. Finish on E. Always.
  5. Test it at training this week, then change whatever didn’t fit.

06

Practise It

Practise it when you don’t need it, so it’s there when you do.

Build it like any other skill

A routine you’ve only read about won’t show up under pressure. In one study, experienced basketballers practised free throws for five weeks, some under pressure and some without. Only the group who practised under pressure kept their accuracy when they were tested under pressure later. So build ACE the way you’d build any skill: slowly first, then at speed, then with some heat on it.

Stage 1 · week 1
Learn it

One full ACE a day somewhere calm. In bed, on the bus. Use the 10 minute timer above.

Stage 2 · week 2
Shrink it

Keep the daily full ACE, and add ten short ACEs at every training, hooked to natural gaps: drinks, between reps, walking back.

Stage 3 · week 3
Pressure it

Run it straight after mistakes at training, and in drills with something on the line: a score, a forfeit, people watching. Ask your coach for a few pressure reps.

Stage 4 · week 4 on
Play it

Use it in competition. After each game, a two minute review: where did I use it, what showed up, what helped, what will I change?

The rep is the return. Your attention will wander off during ACE, sometimes after two seconds. That isn’t you doing it wrong. Noticing that it wandered and bringing it back is the exact skill you’re training. A session full of wandering and returning is a good session.

Activity · ongoing

Your rep log

Log six reps over the next two weeks. A mix of speeds is ideal. Bring this to your next session.

Tap a rep to open it. “Got back to the job” is out of 10: how well you got back to what you were doing, not how calm you felt.

07

When It Doesn’t Go to Plan

The things athletes most often tell us, and what to do about each one.

Every one of these is normal. None of them means ACE isn’t for you.

That’s ACE working. It was never meant to switch the nerves off. The question to ask is: did I get back to the job? If you did, even for a moment, that’s a win.

Nerves before something that matters are a sign that it matters. Most good performances happen with some nerves in the room.

Very common in the first few weeks. Under pressure we fall back on whatever is most practised, and ACE isn’t there yet. Two fixes.

Hook it to a trigger that happens every game (Tool 05). And get more reps at training, especially after mistakes (Tool 06). Then add a review after each game so you catch the moments you missed.

Turn the dial down. If there are five seconds, use the one breath version. If there’s no time at all, because the ball is already coming back at you, just do E: next job. Then run a proper ACE at the next gap.

Of course it did. Minds are built to replay things that went wrong. You don’t need to stop it happening. Notice it, name it, and come back again. Five returns in a minute is five reps.

If one mistake keeps replaying for days, bring it to a session. The reaction replay on the Aware page can help too.

Make it invisible. Nobody needs to see you Acknowledge: it happens in your head. Your Connect move can be pressing your toes into your boots or regripping your stick. Your cue word stays silent. A good ACE looks like nothing at all from the outside.

And plenty of the athletes you look up to are doing something similar. You just can’t see it either.

That happens for some people, and it’s worth listening to. Drop the breath from your Connect step completely. Use your feet, your hands, or your eyes on something fixed outside you. Connect is about landing, and there are lots of ways to land.

If paying attention to your body in general brings up a lot of distress, let your practitioner know, and we’ll find a version that works for you.

That’s the routine starting to run you, and it’s worth catching early. A routine is there to serve you, not to be completed perfectly. Practise it flexibly on purpose: sometimes skip straight to E, sometimes swap your Connect move, sometimes get interrupted halfway and go anyway.

If you’re adding steps over time, or feel you must finish it before you’re allowed to start, talk to us about it.

“Something’s here” is a perfectly good Acknowledge. You can get more precise over time. Notice & name has a naming grid that splits a moment into thoughts, feelings, body and urges, which makes it much easier.

ACE works off the field too: before an exam, a hard conversation, or at 2am when your head won’t stop. But it isn’t meant to carry everything on its own. If life is feeling heavy, bring it to your next session or get in touch sooner. If you need to talk to someone right now, you can call or text 1737 any time to reach a trained counsellor, free.

For TPA practitioners and coaches

Teaching ACE
well

Athletes, you’re welcome to read this too. It’s how we think about teaching ACE, so there are no secrets in here.

This section is for the people teaching and supporting ACE: TPA practitioners, and coaches who work alongside us. Open whichever part you need.

Our first sessions are about formulation, with ACE introduced lightly. This page comes after that. Send it once the athlete has felt ACE in session at least once, not as first session homework. It’s the bridge between the toolkits (Open, Aware, Engaged) and the athlete’s actual competition routine.

A typical order:

  1. Introduce ACE in session using a real recent moment from their sport.
  2. Set the Watch section and Tool 01 between sessions.
  3. Next session: Tool 02, then Tool 03 or Tool 04, whichever suits their sport and where the difficulty shows up (in play, or in the build up).
  4. Build Tool 05 together in session, so the words and moves are genuinely theirs.
  5. Tools 06 and 07 as the reps start. Review the rep log and the after game review each session.

ACE is the towards move side of the work. DOTS (Distract, Opt out, Time travel, Self sabotage) helps athletes spot their away moves. The two sit naturally together on a choice point.

However you adapt ACE for an athlete, keep these five intact. They’re what make it ACE rather than a relaxation routine.

  1. Acknowledge comes first and is never skipped. Without it, ACE drifts into suppression or distraction.
  2. Success is measured by workability, not calm. “Did you get back to the job?” rather than “Did it make the nerves go?”
  3. It always finishes on Engage: the next action, with a cue or a value.
  4. The moves are the same at every speed. Only the dose changes.
  5. The words, moves and cues belong to the athlete, in their own language.

Acknowledge. Use the naming grid (thoughts, feelings, body, urges). Model the language: “I’m noticing…”, “here’s…”. Coach precision over volume. Watch for the athlete debating the thought or seeking reassurance about it. Both are ways of not quite acknowledging.

Connect. Offer a menu (feet, hands, eyes, equipment, breath) and let the athlete choose. Then shape it smaller until it’s invisible in their sport. Default to external options (eyes, feet, equipment) for athletes with a trauma history, panic, or anyone for whom internal body focus raises distress.

Engage. Cue words should be short, action based and pointed outward, and matched to the task: more technical for fine skills like a kick or a putt, more drive based for effort and endurance. Check the cue isn’t a hidden “don’t”. In the long version, link it to the athlete’s values.

Practise it live, with noise. Have the athlete run ACE while you add noise: recall their last mistake, count down out loud, put a pretend scoreboard on, then have them stand up and perform a skill. A rep in a calm room is not the same as a rep with noise in it.

  • Leave gaps in drills and let athletes use them. A few seconds between reps is enough for a short ACE.
  • Add pressure reps once athletes have the basics: a score, a consequence, being watched. That’s where the routine gets tested.
  • Don’t police it. Asking “did you do your ACE?” in front of the team straight after an error adds pressure. Ask privately, later, and with curiosity.
  • Team versions work. A captain’s call word after conceding can act as a shared Engage cue, like the luge pair’s lyric.
  • Your own ACE matters. Athletes read the sideline. A coach who can name their frustration and get back to the job is the best demonstration there is.
  • Rigid ritual. The athlete can’t perform if the routine is interrupted, or keeps adding steps. Work on flexibility: deliberately vary it, and practise skipping straight to Engage.
  • ACE as avoidance. Connect is used to escape (eyes always shut, always leaving the room, always in their headphones). Check the function with the DOTS lens.
  • Chasing calm. The athlete says ACE has “stopped working”. This is usually the off switch trap. Go back to workability.
  • Bigger than sport. Low mood, sleep or eating changes, distress outside sport, or any safety concern: step out of performance mode and follow our clinical and safety processes.

Your ACE routine in seven lines

  • Three moves, always in this order. Acknowledge, Connect, Engage.
  • An anchor, not an off switch. Judge it by whether you got back to the job, not by how calm you felt.
  • Never skip A. Naming it is what stops ACE becoming another escape route.
  • Make C small and yours. Feet, hands, eyes or breath. Nobody needs to see it.
  • Always finish on E. Next job, cue word, go.
  • One routine, four speeds. One breath, forty seconds, a few minutes, ten minutes.
  • Train it long so it works short. Practise it when you don’t need it.
