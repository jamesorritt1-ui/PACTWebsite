Noise in
the boat

Session one was about what you paddle for. Session two was about what to do when the noise shows up anyway. This page holds the ideas and tools from the night, so you can come back to them, try them at training, and share them with the paddler beside you.

Short on time? Jump to your own ACE.

Read it all in about twenty minutes, or dip in where you need to. Anything you write on this page stays on your own device.

01 · Where we picked up

The wind
keeps blowing

We started with some kōrero about how the last few weeks had gone: the strokes you’d been trying, what had stuck with you, and what had slipped. All of it was useful, including the bits that didn’t go to plan.

Last time, the aka matua was what you paddle for, and the wind was the tiredness, nerves and frustration that come along with it. This session was about the wind. Not how to make it stop, but how to keep hold of the vine while it blows. If you want a refresher on your coin, your stroke or open, aware and engaged, session one is all still here.

02 · Open

The more we wriggle,
the deeper we go

We came back to the jam doughnut. Try not to think about one, and it turns up straight away. Most of us know this from real life too: the harder we try not to think about a bad race or a niggle in the shoulder, the more often it pops back into our heads.

That’s when we tend to get into a fight with ourselves. We argue with the thought, try to talk ourselves out of the feeling, or try to distract ourselves from it. It’s a bit like quicksand. The more you thrash about to get free, the further in you sink. Even distraction can turn into its own kind of noise, because part of your mind is busy checking whether the thing you’re avoiding has gone yet.

1
It turns up
A thought, a feeling, a memory of the last race.
2
We fight it
Argue with it, push it down, try to think about anything else.
3
We sink
It comes back louder, and the fight takes our attention.
4
The boat misses out
Less of us left for the stroke, the timing and the crew.

In quicksand, the way out is to stop thrashing, spread out and make room.

03 · Open

You are more than
your thoughts

Our minds are always talking. Most of the time that’s helpful. The trouble starts when we treat every thought or feeling as the truth, as a fact about us, or as an order we have to follow. In ACT this is called fusion: we’re so tangled up with the thought that we can’t see anything else. “I’m too slow.” “I’ll let the crew down.” “This is hopeless.” When we’re fused, these stop sounding like thoughts and start sounding like the way things are.

Defusion, or unhooking, is stepping back half a pace so you can see the thought for what it is: words and pictures your mind is offering you. The thought might still be there. It just isn’t running the boat.

Fused

The thought is the truth

It feels like a fact. It fills your view, decides how you feel, and usually decides what you do next.

Unhooked

The thought is a thought

You can notice it, hold it a little more lightly, and still choose what you do with your next stroke.

Hands as
thoughts

We tried this one in the room. It’s worth doing again at home, because the point lands in your body rather than your head.

  1. Hold both hands up close in front of your face, palms towards you. These are your thoughts. “I can’t be bothered tonight.” “Everyone’s fitter than me.”
  2. Now try to look around the room, or imagine finding the timing in the boat. Notice how much you can actually see.
  3. Slowly lower your hands until they rest in your lap.
  4. Your hands haven’t gone anywhere. What can you see now?

If a shoulder is sore, use one hand, keep it low, or simply picture it.

Unhooking doesn’t get rid of our thoughts. It gets them out of our eyes, so we can see the boat.

Why bother with the metaphors?

Some of these exercises can feel a bit funny or silly, and that’s fine. Laughing at them is part of how they work. But each one has a real point underneath it. When we give ourselves a little space between us and our thoughts, feelings and emotions, we get a chance to see the situation more clearly, to stay connected with the people we love, and to be present for the moments that turn into memories that last a lifetime. On the water, in the tent, and well beyond the boat.

04 · A reflection to take home

When a feeling
took the paddle

In the session we asked you to think of one moment where a feeling or a thought took over for a while. It doesn’t need to be dramatic, and it doesn’t need to be about paddling. A moment in the boat, at work, at home or on the drive somewhere. Bring that one moment to mind, then sit with these questions.

  • When you were hooked by it, what did it take away from you in that moment? What did you miss, or stop doing?
  • What did it pull you to do? What was the urge?
  • What would it have been like to have just a little more space with that thought or feeling, without needing it to go away?
  • With that space, what might you have done, or who might you have been, instead?

There are no right answers here, and nobody else needs to see them. Noticing is the skill. If you’d like to write it down, use the card below. It saves only on this device.

My moment

One moment where a thought or feeling took over. Private to you.

05 · Aware

What mental
noise actually is

Next we each brought to mind a really frustrating situation, the kind that comes up again and again. Then we pulled it apart. Mental noise isn’t one thing. It’s a mix of what our mind says, what we feel, and what happens in our body. And almost always, it comes with a push to do something.

Situation
Something happens
The timing falls apart. Someone’s late. A bad piece.
Noise
Thoughts, emotions, feelings
“Not again.” Frustration. A tight jaw, hot face, heavy arms.
Urge
A pull to act
Snap at someone, go quiet, give up on the piece, stay home next time.
Choice
What we do
This is the part we can work with.

Can we control
all the noise?

Some of it, a little. There are things we can do across our week that turn the volume up or down. When we’re well fuelled, drinking enough water and getting decent sleep, the noise tends to be a bit quieter and easier to work with. When we’re running on empty, it gets louder. Looking after your brain health is worth doing.

But some noise just turns up. Frustration, nerves and worry will still pop up sometimes, however well we’ve slept and eaten. That isn’t a sign anything has gone wrong. It’s being a person in a boat.

Turns the volume

Things that influence the noise

  • Sleep and rest
  • Food and water before training
  • How much else is on your plate this week

Worth looking after. They won’t switch the noise off.

In our hands, in the moment

What we do with the urge

  • Noticing the noise and naming it
  • Coming back to this stroke
  • Choosing the next action that fits what matters

This is where ACE comes in.

We can’t always choose the noise. We can get better at choosing what we do while it’s there.

06 · Open, aware, engaged in the moment

Acknowledge,
connect, engage

ACE is how open, aware and engaged from session one turns into something you can actually do when the noise turns up. It draws on Russ Harris’s “dropping anchor” practice from ACT. An anchor doesn’t stop the storm. It keeps the boat steady while the storm does what it does.

A
Acknowledge

Notice what’s here and name it. Make a little space between you and it.

“Steve’s in the room.”
C
Connect

Come back into your body and this moment. Feet, hands, breath, what you can see and hear.

“Feet in the footbrace.”
E
Engage

Bring your attention to what you’re doing, and take one small step toward what matters.

“Next catch, with the crew.”

ACE is a shape, not a script

There’s no single right way to do it. How you use ACE will change depending on the situation and on you. Some people move through it in about five seconds a step. Others spend a couple of minutes in each part. In the middle of a race it might be three strokes long. Before a big day it might be a few quiet minutes. Under each part there are different tools, and you get to pick the ones that suit you. You’ll see some of those choices below.

07 · A for acknowledge

When Steve
walks in the room

We went back to that frustrating situation and gave the urge a character. In the room, we called him Steve. When Steve walks in, he brings the frustration and the anger with him, and he’s usually pushing us toward something we wouldn’t choose on a calmer day. Steve can be anyone you like. Some people give theirs a silly name, a voice, an outfit, or a place they like to sit in the boat.

In it

“I am angry.”

Say it out loud. The anger is all of you. There’s no gap between you and the feeling, so the feeling tends to do the deciding.

Noticing it

“Steve’s in the room.”

Now say this one. The anger is still there. But you’ve stepped back half a pace, and you’re the one noticing Steve. That little gap is where the choice lives.

That’s the whole point of acknowledging. It isn’t pretending the feeling isn’t there, and it isn’t telling yourself off for having it. It’s seeing it clearly enough that you can decide what happens next.

Milk, milk,
milk

We also tried a very old exercise, first described by the psychologist Edward Titchener more than a hundred years ago. It shows that thoughts are made of words, and words can lose their grip.

  1. Say the word milk once. Notice what comes to mind: cold, white, a cuppa, the fridge.
  2. Now say it out loud, over and over, as fast as you can, for 30 seconds.
  3. Notice what happens to the word. For most people it turns into a funny sound and the meaning drains away.
  4. Try it with a word that sticks for you, like tired or slow. Does it have the same grip afterwards?

Research on this word repetition exercise has found it can reduce how believable and upsetting a sticky thought feels, at least in the short term (Masuda and colleagues, 2004). Keep the word light. You don’t need to practise it with anything heavy.

Pick the tool
that works for you

Acknowledging can look different for different people, and that’s exactly how it should be. You get to decide which tool helps you make space. Try each one a few times before you settle.

Option one

Give it a character

Name your Steve. “Here’s Steve again.” Said kindly, a bit like spotting someone you know across the car park.

Option two

Word repetition

Take the sticky word and say it quickly, out loud or in your head, until it’s just a sound. Good for off the water.

Option three

Simply notice it

“I’m noticing I’m having the thought that I’m too slow.” Or just “frustration’s here.” Quiet, quick, and nobody needs to know.

08 · C for connect

Back into
this stroke

Once you’ve acknowledged what’s here, connect brings you back into your body and into this moment. When Steve has hold of us, we’re usually somewhere else: replaying the last race or worrying about the next one. Connect brings us back to the only stroke we can actually paddle, which is this one.

Why we don’t
skip straight to connect

Plenty of us already have connect skills. A slow breath, a stretch, focusing on something else. So why bother with acknowledge first? Two reasons.

Reason one

Naming it brings the thinking brain online

Putting a feeling into words seems to help settle the alarm. In one well known brain imaging study, labelling an emotion was linked with more activity in the front of the brain, the prefrontal cortex, and less in the amygdala, which responds to threat (Lieberman and colleagues, 2007). Acknowledging gets the part of you that plans and chooses back in the boat.

Reason two

It’s about how the skill is used

This one matters most. If we use a breathing or grounding skill to try to get rid of the noise, it often backfires. We breathe, check whether the frustration has gone, find it hasn’t, and now we’re frustrated that the breathing didn’t work. That’s the quicksand again. Acknowledging first means we’ve already made room for the noise, so connect is about coming back to the moment, not making the feeling disappear.

The test for any connect skill

Ask yourself: am I doing this to get rid of the feeling, or to come back to what I’m doing? The same breath can be either. Only the second one tends to help.

Anchors that are
already in the boat

We finished this part by thinking about connect skills that would work for each of us in the moment. None of these need anything extra. They’re there every training.

Feet

Push into the footbrace. Feel the boat under you.

Hands

Feel the shaft in your grip. A gentle squeeze, then soften.

Breath

Notice one breath out on the recovery. No need to change it.

Eyes and ears

The drum, the back in front of you, the sound of the water.

09 · E for engage

One small move
toward what matters

Engage is where we bring our attention to what we’re doing and take a step that fits what we care about, with Steve still riding along if he wants to. This is where your coin from session one comes back in. What does the front of it look like in the next stroke, the next minute, or the next conversation?

And like the rest of ACE, it bends to fit the moment. Here are three ways it might look.

ACE isn’t a hard drill to get right. It’s a way of steering that you shape to the day.

10 · Engaged, as a crew

Anchors need
clear actions

To finish, we split into two groups and each took a situation that often causes frustration for the crew. Then we worked out what we’d actually do about it.

Here’s why that step matters. An anchor helps a boat hold steady through a storm, but only if it’s actually attached to something. Last session we explored what’s important to us. Values on their own can feel a bit floaty. The gap is often between knowing what matters and knowing what that looks like on a Thursday night or on race day. Clear behaviours close that gap. They’re what the crew can see, practise, and count on.

One we worked
through together

The frustration
Time management. Not knowing where to be or when, especially on race day.
What it means to us
Being on time is one of the ways we show we’re one crew.
The value underneath
Togetherness
The crew behaviour
A clear time schedule goes up in the tent on race day, so everyone knows where they need to be and when.
What each paddler does
Spend a good couple of minutes actually reading it when you arrive, so you know your times.

Small and specific on purpose. Anyone walking into the tent could see whether it’s happening.

11 · What the research says about strong crews

Clear on what,
who, and what if

A behaviour only holds if people know what’s expected, someone keeps it alive, and the crew knows what happens when it doesn’t go to plan. None of that needs to be heavy. Here’s what the evidence suggests, in plain terms.

1

Make expectations clear and visible

Research on sport teams has found that when people are unsure what’s expected of them, or what happens if they don’t do it, they tend to feel less satisfied in their role (Eys and colleagues, 2003; Beauchamp and colleagues, 2005). Unspoken expectations are where frustration grows. Write the agreement down and put it where the crew will see it, like the schedule in the tent.

2

Share the leading

Leadership in teams doesn’t have to sit with one person. Studies of sports teams describe four kinds of athlete leader: task (on the water), motivational (lifting energy), social (looking after people) and external (the link to the club and organisers). The captain is rarely the best at all four, and teams where athletes rate their peer leadership highly tend to work better together (Fransen and colleagues, 2014, 2017). Ask who’s naturally good at keeping each agreement going, and invite them to lead it.

3

Decide it together

The Prosocial approach, developed by Paul Atkins, David Sloan Wilson and Steven Hayes, builds on Elinor Ostrom’s Nobel prize winning work on how groups manage shared things fairly. Two of its core principles are a shared sense of identity and purpose, and fair, inclusive decision making (Atkins, Wilson and Hayes, 2019). People keep agreements they helped make. Which is exactly why the crew built these actions in groups, rather than being handed them.

4

Keep an eye on it, lightly

Another Prosocial principle is that groups check in on how their agreed behaviours are going. Not policing, just noticing. A quick “how did the schedule work today?” at the debrief does the job, and it’s a good time to notice when it went well, too.

5

When it isn’t met, start small and stay kind

Ostrom’s work found that groups do best when responses to a slip are graduated: start gentle, and only step up if it keeps happening. Things also go better when there’s a quick and fair way to sort out disagreements before they fester (Atkins, Wilson and Hayes, 2019; Wilson and colleagues, 2020). For a crew, that might look like this:

  • First, a friendly reminder. Most slips are someone being busy, tired or unsure, not someone who doesn’t care.
  • If it keeps happening, a quiet chat, curious rather than critical: “What got in the way?” Often the agreement needs tweaking, not the person.
  • If it’s a crew wide pattern, bring it back to the group and review the agreement together.
  • Talk about the behaviour, not the person. Then come back to the value it serves: we do this because we’re one crew.
6

Shape it as when, then

Plans written as “when this happens, we do that” are one of the most consistently supported tools in behaviour change research. They make it much more likely that a good intention actually turns into action (Gollwitzer and Sheeran, 2006). “When we arrive at the tent, we read the schedule.” Simple, and it works because the cue is built in.
