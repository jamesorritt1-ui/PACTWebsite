What this is

A workshop,
not a waiting room

You already have the sessions. This is the bit in between — the place where the work actually sticks.

Every skill in here comes from Acceptance and Commitment Training (ACT) and the wider performance-psychology evidence base. ACT has more than three thousand published studies behind it, and it is used by professional teams, Olympic athletes, armed forces and health services around the world. It is not about thinking positive, or getting rid of nerves, or hardening up. It is about one specific, trainable capacity: psychological flexibility — being able to do what matters to you, even while difficult thoughts and feelings are showing up.

That capacity predicts more than performance. It predicts how you handle study, injury, selection, relationships, and the days when sport is not going your way. That dual relevance — on the field and off it — is the whole point.

The old playbook

  • Don’t show weakness
  • Get rid of the nerves before you go
  • Think positive, block the doubt out
  • Push through, no matter what
  • Confidence first, action second

What actually works

  • Name what’s there — accurately
  • Let the nerves come; they’re fuel, not faults
  • Notice the doubt without obeying it
  • Read the signal, then choose your response
  • Action first; confidence is the by-product
The definition we work from

“Real toughness is experiencing discomfort or distress, leaning in, paying attention, and creating space to take thoughtful action. It’s navigating discomfort to make the best decision you can.”

— Steve Magness, Do Hard Things (2022)

The spine of everything in here

Open. Aware.
Engaged.

Psychological flexibility is built from six processes. Grouped into three pillars — the TriFlex — they become something you can actually hold in your head under pressure.

Open
Acceptance + Defusion

Make room for what shows up — nerves, doubt, anger, the urge to quit — instead of fighting it or obeying it. Let your thoughts be thoughts rather than orders.

Aware
Present moment + Perspective

Put your attention where you choose, when you choose. Come back to now, and to the part of you that simply notices — the part that is bigger than any story about you.

Engaged
Values + Committed action

Know what you stand for, and keep taking the next small step in that direction — on the good days, and especially on the ones where you don’t feel like it.

And the 30-second version: ACE

A — Acknowledge what’s showing up. C — Connect with your body. E — Engage in what you’re doing.

Three moves that map straight onto the three pillars. You can run them on the start line, in the sin bin, before a hard conversation, or at 2am when your head won’t stop. One skill set, every arena. (Adapted with credit from Russ Harris’s “dropping anchor”.)

If you have been through THRIVE, this will look familiar — it is the same spine. The difference is that THRIVE is a nine-week programme you work through in order. This is not. This is a library you dip into, in whatever order your season and your sessions demand.

How to use it

Every skill is built
the same way

So you always know what you’re getting and how long it will take.

  1. What it is, in plain English. Two minutes of reading. No jargon that isn’t explained.
  2. Why it works. The short version of the evidence, with the source named so you can go and check it.
  3. How to do it. Numbered steps you could follow in a changing room.
  4. A worked sport example. Someone actually using it, so you can see the shape of it.
  5. The activity. One clear thing to complete. This is the bit that matters — it saves straight to your journal.
  6. Take it into the week. The smallest real-world rep to try before we next meet.

Reading is not practising. Russ Harris tells a story about a client who threw his book on the table and said “it didn’t work” — then admitted she hadn’t done a single exercise. These are skills, like a set piece or a stroke correction. They only load in through reps.

Your baseline

Before you go anywhere else, take a snapshot of where you are right now. In a few weeks you’ll come back to this and it’ll tell you something honest. There are no right answers and nobody else sees it.

Activity · 01

Where are you right now?

Score each one as it’s actually been this past week, not as you’d like it to be. 0 = not at all. 10 = completely.

OPEN — When difficult thoughts and feelings show up, I can let them be there without fighting them or being run by them.

5

AWARE — I can put my attention where I want it, and bring it back when it wanders.

5

ENGAGED — I know what I stand for, and my actions this week matched it.

5

BRAIN HEALTH — Sleep, food, water, connection: my foundations have been solid this week.

5

What brought you here? What’s the thing you’d most like to be different in three months?

Be specific and be honest. This is the one your practitioner will want to talk about.

Where this comes from

Sources

Nothing in this area is made up. Every skill is adapted, with credit, from published clinical and performance psychology. If you want to go deeper, these are the books and papers we work from — and they are worth reading in full.

The core texts
  1. Harris, R. (2022). The Happiness Trap: How to Stop Struggling and Start Living (2nd ed.). Shambhala. — The plain-English source for dropping anchor (ACE), the choice point, unhooking, TAME, urge surfing, kind hands, values work and the HARD barriers.
  2. Walser, R. D., & Westrup, D. (2019). Learning ACT: An Acceptance and Commitment Therapy Skills Training Manual for Therapists (2nd ed.). Context Press / New Harbinger. — The six flexibility processes, the three pillars (Open, Aware, Engaged), and the therapeutic stance behind how we work with you.
  3. Magness, S. (2022). Do Hard Things: Why We Get Resilience Wrong and the Surprising Science of Real Toughness. HarperOne. — Real toughness, emotions as messengers, distanced self-talk, zooming in and out, and coping flexibility.
  4. Bernardi, G., Fitzgerald, M., & Zilcha-Mano, S. (2018). ACT in Sport: Improve Performance through Mindfulness, Acceptance and Commitment. Dark River. — The sport-specific application: passengers on the bus, the breath-hold experiment, the reaction replay, the rehearsal room, the coin, and the friction principle.
  5. Strosahl, K. D., Robinson, P. J., & Gustavsson, T. (2015). Inside This Moment: A Clinician’s Guide to Promoting Radical Change Using Acceptance and Commitment Therapy. Context Press. — The five phases of present-moment awareness (notice, name, let go, soften, expand) behind The Five Moves. Same authors as the Open/Aware/Engaged grouping.
  6. Biegel, G. M., & Corbin, T. (2018). Mindfulness for Student Athletes: A Workbook to Help Teens Reduce Stress and Enhance Performance. Instant Help / New Harbinger. — The sport-specific practices in The Practice Room: the 9-second check, CPR under pressure, the 2BSET sweep, sense mapping and visual anchors.
  7. Wolf, C., & Serpa, J. G. (2015). A Clinician’s Guide to Teaching Mindfulness. New Harbinger. — How these practices are led and troubleshot: the returning-is-the-rep framing, backdraft, and the obstacles section.
  8. Ciarrochi, J., & Bailey, A. (2008). A CBT Practitioner’s Guide to ACT. New Harbinger. — The flexibility model, the “don’t-get-eaten machine”, and the bridge between traditional CBT and ACT.
  9. Hayes, S. C., Strosahl, K. D., & Wilson, K. G. (2012). Acceptance and Commitment Therapy: The Process and Practice of Mindful Change (2nd ed.). Guilford Press. — The primary scientific account of the model.
Supporting research referenced across the toolkits
  1. A-Tjak, J. G. L., et al. (2015). A meta-analysis of the efficacy of acceptance and commitment therapy for clinically relevant mental and physical health problems. Psychotherapy and Psychosomatics, 84(1), 30–36.
  2. Kross, E., et al. (2014). Self-talk as a regulatory mechanism: How you do it matters. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 106(2), 304–324.
  3. Sheppes, G., et al. (2011). Emotion-regulation choice. Psychological Science, 22(11), 1391–1396.
  4. Lieberman, M. D., et al. (2007). Putting feelings into words: Affect labelling disrupts amygdala activity in response to affective stimuli. Psychological Science, 18(5), 421–428.
  5. Wenzlaff, R. M., & Wegner, D. M. (2000). Thought suppression. Annual Review of Psychology, 51, 59–91.
  6. Marlatt, G. A., & Gordon, J. R. (1985). Relapse Prevention. Guilford Press. — The original urge-surfing work.
  7. Walker, M. (2017). Why We Sleep. Scribner; and Watson, A. M. (2017). Sleep and athletic performance. Current Sports Medicine Reports, 16(6), 413–418.
  8. Ryan, R. M., & Deci, E. L. (2017). Self-Determination Theory. Guilford Press. — Autonomy, competence and relatedness.
  9. Holt-Lunstad, J., et al. (2015). Loneliness and social isolation as risk factors for mortality. Perspectives on Psychological Science, 10(2), 227–237.
