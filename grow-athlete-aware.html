Before you start

You live here.
There is nowhere else.

Every ball you will ever strike, every decision you will ever make, every conversation that will ever matter — all of it happens in the present moment. There is no other venue.

And yet most of us spend an enormous amount of time somewhere else: replaying the error from six minutes ago, or rehearsing a disaster that hasn’t happened. Both feel productive. Neither is. When the conceptualised past or future dominates, your behaviour gets driven by old conditioning rather than by what’s actually in front of you — so you get more of what you already did. New options are foreclosed.

Aware is the middle pillar for a reason. It is not just a target in its own right — it is the method that makes the other two possible. You cannot make room for a feeling you haven’t noticed, and you cannot act on your values in a moment you’re not present for.

Two capacities, not one

This pillar trains two related things. Flexible attention: putting your focus where the situation needs it, holding it there, and moving it when the situation changes. And perspective: contacting the part of you that does the noticing — a vantage point that contains your thoughts and feelings without being any of them.

Why this pillar exists. In the ACT model, inflexible attention and attachment to a conceptualised self combine into the inflexibility pillar called Mindless. Flexible attention plus a perspective-taking sense of self is the antidote — and it is described as the central pillar of ACT’s intervention method, because it makes deliberate change possible at all. Walser & Westrup, Learning ACT (2019), ch. 1, 4 & 5.

01

Dropping Anchor — ACE

For: an emotional storm. When you’re being swept along and you need to hold steady.

What it is

You’re sailing into harbour and a storm is coming in over the radio. You drop anchor, fast and deep. The anchor does not control the weather. It holds the boat steady until the storm passes.

That is exactly what this skill does. Three moves, thirty seconds to three minutes, run in a cycle:

  1. A — Acknowledge your thoughts and feelings. Ten to twenty seconds noticing what’s in your head, then a slow scan head to toe. Name what you find: “here’s a feeling of dread”, “I’m noticing thoughts about not being ready”.
  2. C — Connect with your body. Keep acknowledging and move. Push your feet into the ground. Straighten your spine. Press your palms together. Roll your shoulders. Slowly, gently, deliberately.
  3. E — Engage in what you’re doing. Widen out. Five things you can see. Three you can hear. What you can smell. Then bring your full attention back to the task in front of you.

Then do it again. And again. Three or four cycles is usually enough.

Why it works

Four things happen at once. You get control of your physical actions back — which is the bit that matters most, because it means you can act effectively while the storm is still raging. You reduce the influence of your thoughts and feelings, because consciously noticing them takes away much of their automatic pull. You interrupt worrying and rumination, which are the engines that keep storms going. And you refocus on the task, which is why the exercise always ends by returning to what you’re doing.

If your pain is mild to moderate, it will often fade as you do this. Enjoy that — but never aim for it.

This is not distraction. Distraction means drawing your attention away from what you don’t want to feel. Dropping anchor does the opposite — it starts by actively turning towards it. Skip the Acknowledge step and you haven’t got an anchor, you’ve got another escape route. And you already have plenty of those.

Worked example · a hockey player, 2–0 down at half time

A: “Chest is tight. Head’s doing the ‘we’ve blown this’ thing. Legs feel heavy.”
C: Both feet flat into the turf, push down. Straighten up. Roll the shoulders once.
E: The scoreboard. The coach’s whiteboard. The sound of the away crowd. Her stick in her hands. Then: what’s the first job in the second half?

Twenty-five seconds. Three cycles across the break. She still felt sick about the score. She also heard the entire team talk, which she would not have done otherwise.

02

Notice & Name

For: “I don’t know what’s wrong, I just feel off.”

What it is

The foundation skill. It sits underneath every other tool in this whole area, and it’s two words long: notice what’s showing up, and name it, without judging it.

The naming matters more than it sounds. There is a real difference between these two sentences:

Fused

  • “I’m angry.”
  • “I’m a bottler.”
  • “I’m anxious.”

Named

  • “I’m noticing anger.”
  • “I’m noticing the thought that I’m a bottler.”
  • “Here’s anxiety.”

In the left column you are the thing. In the right column it is something passing through you. That gap — and it takes about a second to create — is where every other skill you’ll learn gets its foothold.

Why it works

Think of a classroom when the teacher walks out. Chaos. Then the teacher comes back and everyone settles — not because they were told off, but because someone is watching. Your awareness works the same way on your inner world. Thoughts and feelings you aren’t consciously aware of run wild and pull you around. The moment you notice and name them, they lose a big chunk of that power.

Underneath the metaphor is a well-replicated finding: putting feelings into words engages the prefrontal cortex and dampens amygdala reactivity. This is why the very first move in almost every evidence-based emotion skill is name it.

Magness makes the same point from the performance side: emotions are messengers, not dictators. A signal you can’t read is just noise that pushes you around. Accurate naming turns it into information you can use.

Precision beats volume. “Bad” and “stressed” are not names, they’re categories. There is a large difference between dread, resentment, embarrassment and grief — and each one calls for a different response. Athletes who can only report “fine” or “not great” are working with a two-colour palette.

03

Zoom In, Zoom Out

For: tunnel vision, spiralling, or being so wide open you can’t lock onto anything.

What it is

Attention has a zoom lens, and under stress it defaults to narrow. That is not a fault — it’s useful. Narrowing directs all your processing power at the thing that matters and shuts out distraction. Elite marathoners deliberately zoom right in on cadence, breathing, the burn in the quads.

The problem is staying narrow too long. Pilots landing in heavy crosswinds zoom in so hard that in one study 40% missed a loud alarm going off in the cockpit. Scientists call it cognitive narrowing; pilots call it inattentional deafness. When you’re stuck narrow, you miss signals, you stop seeing options, and — critically — rumination takes hold. One group of psychologists argue that rumination essentially is a narrow, inflexible focus of attention: you can’t break away from the one thing screaming loudest.

The skill is not being narrow or broad. It’s being able to move.

Narrow Broad

Why it works

Shifting your attention drags everything else with it. Narrow your focus and your thoughts and mood follow it down. Go broad — and the research is consistent here — and creativity, problem-solving and mood all lift. In one study, simply asking people to look at a whole map rather than one highlighted city measurably improved their performance on creative problem-solving straight afterwards.

That’s why the antidote to a spiral is almost always the same: if you’re getting narrow, counteract by going broad.

Zoom in when

You’re about to execute. The lineout call. The free throw. The last 400m. You need one thing, sharp, and everything else gone.

Zoom out when

You’re replaying an error. You’ve been stuck on one thought for ten minutes. You’ve stopped seeing options. You’re snapping at people.

Worked example · a rower, 900m in

Narrow is right for the first 1500 — catch, drive, breathe, repeat. But at 900m he made a bad blade and got stuck on it: that’s the race gone, I’ve cost us this. Narrow focus, negative content, and the boat slowing while his mind chews on a stroke that has already happened.

His cue is one word — “wide” — and it means: lift the eyes to the far end of the course, hear the cox, feel the whole boat rather than his own handle. Two strokes wide. Then back down to narrow, on the next catch. He doesn’t stay broad. He just breaks the lock.

04

The Noticing Self

For: when you feel like you are the doubt, the anger, the slump.

What it is

There are two parts of you at work, and telling them apart changes everything.

The thinking self thinks. It plans, judges, compares, remembers, predicts, criticises and daydreams. When we say “your mind” in this area, this is what we mean.

The noticing self doesn’t think at all. It only notices. It’s the part responsible for attention and awareness — and it has been there your whole life, unchanged, watching every version of you that has ever existed.

Here’s the sporting version. When you’re truly locked in and the ball is coming at you, all your attention is riveted on it. That’s the noticing self at work — you are not thinking about the ball, you are noticing it. Then a thought arrives: “don’t drop this”. That’s the thinking self. And you already know what happens to your hands when you start attending to that instead.

Why it works

Your thinking self is a radio you cannot switch off. Not even meditation masters can turn it off, and the harder you try, the louder it plays. Mostly it broadcasts the Doom and Gloom Show: past pain, future danger, and a running commentary on everything wrong with you.

But you already know how to have a radio on in the background without listening to it. That’s the move. Not silence — just not tuning in. If the broadcast is useful, listen and use it. If it isn’t, let it play while you get on with what you’re doing.

And note what this isn’t: positive thinking is bringing a second radio into the room, tuned to Happy and Joyful, and turning it up to drown out the first. Try focusing on anything with two radios blaring.

Worked example · a tennis player between points

“My thinking self spent the whole third set telling me I’d thrown it away. That never stopped. What changed is that I stopped treating it as a briefing and started treating it as commentary from a bloke I hadn’t hired. It was still on. I just wasn’t taking notes.”

The Documentary of You

For: “I’m just not a big-game player.” The identity stuff. The stories that feel like facts.

What it is

Think of a documentary about a country you’ve never been to. Brilliantly shot, three hours long, packed with detail. It is still not the country. It can’t give you the heat, the food, the sound of the street, the people. A documentary about a place is not the place.

Your mind is the most prolific documentary maker there has ever been. It has been filming you 24 hours a day since you were born — by thirty, that’s over a quarter of a million hours of footage. And out of all that, it keeps a fraction of one percent, cuts it into a story, and announces: “This is you. This is who you are.”

Usually with the subtitle: not good enough.

Now, all documentaries are biased — a one-hour TV doc is edited down from hundreds of hours for maximum dramatic effect, reflecting the view of whoever made it. Yours is no different. And the problem is not that the documentary exists. It’s that you think you are it.

Why the usual fix doesn’t work

The standard advice is to build high self-esteem: focus on your strengths, tell yourself you’re good, hold that positive image tight. Most athletes have tried this. Four things go wrong.

  1. You can’t convince your mind. You marshal the evidence, you nearly believe it, and your mind replies: “yeah, but deep down you know the truth.”
  2. It’s exhausting. You now have to keep proving it, forever, and there’s a whole army of self-judgments waiting for you to slip.
  3. Affirmations don’t land. If you don’t believe it, saying it louder doesn’t help. And the research is clear that positive self-talk backfires in exactly the people who need it most — those with low self-esteem to begin with. Your brain is not fooled by false bravado.
  4. Positives attract negatives. Say “I’m a good player” and your mind immediately produces the counter-example. Every time.

What you end up with is fragile self-esteem: fine while you’re performing, catastrophic when you’re not. It’s extremely common in high achievers, and it drives the cycle of pressure, stress and burnout.

The alternative is not a better documentary. It’s holding the whole thing lightly — the flattering bits as well as the brutal ones — and getting on with what matters.

Hold the good reviews lightly too. If your mind starts running “I’m the best in this squad”, that’s the same machinery, and it comes with its own costs — overconfidence, poor preparation, and a very long fall. Thanks, mind. Noted. Back to work.

Distanced Self-Talk

For: the spiral. When you’re too close to it to think straight.

What it is

“It’s easier to give advice to a friend than to yourself.” Everyone knows this is true. Your mate agonises for a month over a decision you could call in four seconds.

It turns out you can manufacture that effect deliberately, and the switch is astonishingly small: change the pronoun.

Instead of “I can do this” — “You can do this”. Or use your own name: “Sam, you’ve trained for this.” First person locks you inside the experience. Second and third person put a gap between you and it.

Why it works

The research here is unusually clean. In one study, six-year-olds were given a boring task with a tempting iPad nearby. Those who reflected in first person (“am I working hard?”) stayed on task about 35% of the time. Those who used their own name managed about 45%. Those who asked “is Batman working hard?” managed nearly 60%. The further from self-immersion, the longer they persisted.

The same paradigm has been run with adults across public speaking, high-stakes decisions and distressing memories, and it holds: distanced self-talk lowers anxiety, shame and rumination, and improves performance. Experts rate the speeches as better. People make more fact-based decisions. Brain-imaging work shows reduced activity in self-referential processing when people use third-person language about negative memories.

The mechanism: a self-immersed perspective amplifies emotion, narrows your world, and makes you read the situation as a threat. A self-distanced perspective broadens it and lets you read the same situation as a challenge.

And one more useful finding: saying it out loud works better than saying it internally. External speech is a simpler, older system — it cuts through when your inner voice has stopped being listened to.

Fair beats positive. This is not about forcing cheerfulness. As the philosopher Alain de Botton puts it, a good internal voice is like a genuinely decent judge: able to separate good from bad, but always merciful, fair, accurate about what’s going on, and interested in helping you deal with it. The question isn’t whether your inner voice is an optimist or a pessimist. It’s whether it’s fair.

Your Reset Routine

For: the ten seconds after a mistake. Building all of the above into one repeatable move.

What it is

Everything in this pillar so far has been a separate skill. This is where you weld them into one thing you can run in the ten seconds between plays — without thinking about it.

A reset has four parts, in this order:

  1. The release. A physical action that marks the end of the last play. Adjust the strapping, tug the sock, wipe the hands on the shorts, one hard exhale. Small, invisible to everyone else, and always the same.
  2. The name. One or two words for what just showed up. “Frustration.” “There’s the Choker story.” One second. Don’t skip it — without it this is just a fidget.
  3. The anchor. Feet into the ground. Spine up. Contact with your body.
  4. The refocus cue. One external thing to lock onto for the next play. The ball, the mark, the target, the next job.

Release. Name. Anchor. Refocus. Four beats, about eight seconds.

Why it works

Pre-performance and between-play routines are among the best-supported interventions in applied sport psychology — but only when they do something. A superstition is a routine with the working parts removed. This one has all four: it interrupts rumination, it names the experience (which lowers reactivity), it returns physical control, and it points attention at something external and task-relevant.

Do the same four beats every time and the sequence becomes automatic. That matters enormously, because the moment you need it most is the moment you have least capacity to construct it. Under real pressure you don’t rise to the occasion; you fall back on what you’ve trained.

Worked example · three athletes, same four beats

Goalkeeper, after conceding: picks up the ball and hands it to the ref (release) · “anger” (name) · both feet into the six-yard box, stands tall (anchor) · eyes to the halfway line, calls the shape (refocus).

Golfer, after a bad drive: replaces the headcover and steps off the tee (release) · “here’s the ‘here we go again’ story” (name) · feels the grip and the ground through the shoes (anchor) · picks a target for the next shot before leaving the tee (refocus).

Sprinter, after a poor start: one hard exhale, shakes the hands out (release) · “panic” (name) · drives the feet into the track (anchor) · eyes to lane 4, the far line (refocus).

Activity · 10 minutes + rehearsal

Build your reset

Write your four beats. Then rehearse them — at training, not in a game. A reset you’ve only ever read is not a reset.

The moment this reset is for

Be specific. Different moments may need different resets, but start with one.

Beat 1 — The release

One physical action. Small enough that nobody notices, consistent enough that your body learns it.

Beat 2 — The name

The one or two words you’ll most often need. You can always change it in the moment.

Beat 3 — The anchor

Beat 4 — The refocus cue

The Reaction Replay

For: finding out where your attention actually goes when it matters — not where you think it goes.

What it is

Take one moment — big or trivial — and break your reaction into five separate channels. Then look at how much you were able to write in each one.

That last part is the whole tool, and it is where it stops being a worksheet and starts being a diagnostic.

The five channels
  1. The trigger. What set it off — something outside you, or something inside like a memory or a twinge?
  2. What your senses picked up. Everything from outside your skin: what you could see, hear, smell, taste, feel through touch. The physical world, as it actually was.
  3. What your mind said. Thoughts, judgements, predictions, images, comparisons, the running commentary.
  4. What your body did. Sensations from inside: tightness, heat, churn, heaviness, tingling. Physical words only.
  5. What you did. Behaviour. Anything a camera would have caught.
Why it works — and what it nearly always shows

Do this once and something predictable happens. Channel three — what your mind said — fills up. Channels two and four come out nearly empty.

A golfer replaying a bad tee shot can write half a page of judgements and predictions. Ask what he could see and he says: “well, I must have seen the ball. The grass would have been green.” Must have. Would have. He wasn’t there. His attention was almost entirely inside his own commentary, at the exact moment he needed to be reading a shot.

That is not a personal failing. It is what attention does under pressure, in everyone. But it explains something important: catastrophic drops in performance are strongly linked to attention shifting off the game and onto self-evaluation. Not to lack of skill. To where you were looking.

So the aim isn’t to think less. It’s to turn up the volume on channels two and four — the physical world and your actual body — so that your mind's commentary stops being the only thing in the room.

“Embarrassed” is not a body sensation. It’s a summary word for a bundle of sensations plus a judgement. Push past it: face hot, shoulders drawing forward, stomach dropping, throat tight. Same for “my face went red” — unless you had a mirror, that’s a conclusion, not something you felt. The precision is the practice.

The Expectation Ladder

For: the standard you never agreed to, never said out loud, and quietly fail against every week.

What it is

Most athletes can tell you their goals. Almost none can tell you their expectations — and expectations are what actually determine whether a performance feels like a success or a quiet disappointment.

The difference matters. A goal is something you chose, out loud, and could write down. An expectation is a vague, unspoken sense of what you should be able to do — and because it was never stated, it never gets examined, and it drifts upward without ever being checked.

So you play a solid game, hit your actual goals, and still drive home flat. That gap has a cause, and this exercise drags it into the light by making it ridiculous.

Why it works

You build a ladder of outcomes for one upcoming event. Rung one is fine. Rung two is a bit better. And you keep climbing until it’s absurd — national call-up, sponsorship, the house on the beach.

Then you mark where the disappointment actually starts.

Almost everyone discovers their line sits several rungs above what they’d have said their goal was, and often somewhere faintly comical. Seeing your private standard written between “played well” and “buys an island” does something no amount of reasoning does — it makes it visible as a story rather than felt as a fact.

To be clear: this is not about wanting less. Ambition is fine, and desire drives performance. What we’re loosening is fusion with an unexamined standard — the thing that turns a good day into a failure and takes your attention off the job.

Worked example · a club golfer’s ladder

1. Turns up, no disasters, plays roughly to handicap.
2. Better than usual. A few decent holes.
3. Best round of the season. Someone mentions it in the bar.
4. Best round ever. Wins the club championship.
5. Invited to play in a pro-am.
6. Wins a major.
7. Buys a mansion in Hawaii.

His stated goal was rung one. When he was honest about where the flatness kicked in, it was rung three. He had been quietly grading himself against the best round of his season, every single week, and calling the resulting mood “not being satisfied easily”.

Activity · 8 minutes

Build your ladder, then find your line

Pick one event coming up. Build at least six rungs and let the top ones get genuinely silly — the absurdity is doing real work, not providing comic relief.

1. The event

2. Your stated goal — what you’d tell your coach

3. Build the ladder — lowest rung first, climbing to ridiculous

4. Now the honest one: which rung is the lowest you could hit and still drive home happy?

Not which one you’d say. The one where the flatness actually starts.

3

5. What’s the gap between your stated goal and your actual line?

If they’re the same rung, brilliant — and unusual. If there’s a gap, that gap is where your flat Sundays come from.

Saved

6. Name it, so you can spot it next time

Give the unspoken standard a title, the way you named your story in the Open toolkit.

Take it into the week

After your next event, before you decide how it went, ask one question: “which rung am I marking this against — my goal, or my rule?” You don’t have to change the answer. Just notice which one you used.

The whole pillar in nine lines

  • Now is the only venue. Everything that matters happens here.
  • Anchors don’t stop storms. They hold you steady while the storm does what storms do.
  • Name it accurately. “Bad” is not a name. Precision buys you options.
  • Stuck narrow? Go broad. Attention drags thought and mood along behind it.
  • You are not your thinking self. You’re the part that notices it.
  • The documentary isn’t you. Hold the bad reviews and the good ones equally lightly.
  • Change the pronoun. The smallest intervention in this whole area, and one of the best evidenced.
  • Turn up the volume on the physical world. Your commentary is loud enough already.
  • Know which rung you’re grading yourself against. It’s rarely the one you’d name out loud.
