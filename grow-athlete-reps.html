What just happened

Most people get the count right. A lot of people also miss things that happened right in front of them.

In the original version of this experiment, about half of the people watching never saw the gorilla walk into the middle of the game, stop, and beat its chest. In this newer version, plenty of people who already knew about the gorilla did spot it, and then missed the curtain changing colour and a player in black walking off the court. Knowing there was a trick didn’t help them see the rest.

That isn’t a flaw in you. It’s how attention works. Attention is a spotlight, not a floodlight. Whatever it’s pointed at gets seen in detail. A lot of what it isn’t pointed at doesn’t get seen at all.

Now swap the basketball for your day

If your attention is always counting the passes that the “not good enough” voice cares about (the dropped ball, the missed shot, what you should have done better, what still needs fixing) it gets very good at seeing those. It’s been trained to.

Meanwhile the good stuff walks straight through the middle of your day, beats its chest and walks off unseen. The drill where something finally clicked. The pass you made under pressure. The mate who picked you up after training. It all happened. You just weren’t counting it.

Counting passes isn’t wrong. Athletes need to look at what went wrong, and your coach will make sure you do. The problem is when it’s the only thing your attention ever counts. Three Good Reps is ten minutes a day of deliberately looking for the gorilla.

Linked skill

Being stuck narrow on one thing is a pattern you’ve met before. Zoom in, zoom out in the Aware toolkit is about moving your attention on purpose. This page is a daily version of zooming out, aimed at the part of your day you usually miss.

How it works

Three reps.
One savoured.

At the end of each day, or after training, you write down three things. Each one trains your attention to catch something it usually misses.

1
A rep I’d want to see again

Builds belief from evidence

One thing you did today that you’d want on the highlights reel. Described as what you did, like a clip, not who you are.

2
A rep I did anyway

Doing it with the noise there

One thing you did while nerves, doubt or tiredness were still there. You name the noise, then the action.

3
Something I was given

Gratitude, made specific

One person or thing that made today better, and what exactly they did.

Then savour one.Pick one of the three and replay it for a minute. Where you were, what you could see and hear, what it felt like in your body. Let it land before you move on.

This is not positive thinking. You’re not telling yourself you’re great, and you’re not pretending the hard stuff didn’t happen. Your mind wouldn’t buy it anyway (that’s The Documentary of You). You’re keeping an honest record of what you actually did. Confidence that rests on evidence of what you did, especially what you did while the doubt was loud, holds up far better than confidence that rests on feeling good or on what other people say.

Four rules

Rule 1
The bar is low on purpose

If it happened and it helped, it counts. “I turned up” is a rep. When the bar for “good” is set too high, you almost never see yourself succeed, and confidence drops along with it.

Rule 2
What you did, not who you are

“I kept calling for the ball after my first drop”, not “I’m a strong player.” Actions are evidence you can repeat. Labels fall over the first time you have a bad day.

Rule 3
It isn’t a positivity test

Hard stuff can sit right next to your reps. A rubbish day with one honest rep in it is a good entry.

Rule 4
No streaks

Miss a day and nothing is lost. There’s nothing to keep alive. Just pick it up tonight.

Why it works

Your own successes are the strongest source of self belief there is. Belief that you can do something (self efficacy) grows mainly from evidence that you’ve done it, and most of that evidence goes unnoticed unless you look for it.

Noticing good attempts helps you learn. In motor learning experiments, people who got feedback after their better attempts learned more, and felt more capable, than people who got it after their worst ones. When the bar for a good attempt was set too high, people hardly ever saw themselves succeed, and both their confidence and their learning dropped. That’s why the bar on this page is low.

Wording matters. Children praised for what they had just done (good kicks) handled a later setback better than children praised for who they were (a great player). That’s rule 2.

Your past self is the fairest comparison. Seeing your own improvement over time helped people learn and eased their nerves. That’s what the Sunday look back is for.

Gratitude is good for you. Writing down what you’re grateful for has been linked with better wellbeing and better sleep, and researchers have started to link gratitude with mental toughness as well. To be honest, there isn’t yet good evidence that it lifts performance directly. It’s here because it’s good for you and for the people around you.

Athletes who only ever review what went wrong tend to end up with an accurate list of faults and very little belief they can fix them. Seeing what you’re already doing well is what gives you the confidence to go after the rest.

Three Good Reps in six lines

  • Attention is a spotlight. Whatever it counts, it sees.
  • Look for the gorilla on purpose. Three reps, one savoured, ten minutes.
  • What you did, not who you are. Write it like a clip.
  • The bar is low on purpose. “I turned up” counts.
  • The rep you did anyway is the one that builds the most.
  • No streaks. Miss a day, pick it up tonight.
