What just happened

How many did you get? Most people get the count about right. What a lot of people miss is everything else going on in front of them.

In the original experiment, about half the people watching never saw the gorilla at all. It walks into the middle of the game, stops, beats its chest and walks off. In this newer version, people who’d heard about the gorilla spotted it fine, then missed the curtain changing colour and one of the black team leaving the court. Knowing there was a trick didn’t help them see the rest.

There’s nothing wrong with you. Attention works like a spotlight. Whatever it’s pointed at, you see in detail, and a lot of what sits outside the beam you never see at all.

Now swap the basketball for your day

Most athletes spend the day counting the passes the “not good enough” voice cares about. The dropped ball. The shot you should have taken. Do that for long enough and you get very good at spotting them.

Meanwhile the good stuff walks through the middle of your day, beats its chest and wanders off without you clocking it. The drill where something finally clicked. The mate who gave you a lift home. It all happened. You just weren’t counting it.

You still need to look at what went wrong, and your coach will make sure you do. The trouble starts when that’s all you ever count. Three Good Reps is ten minutes a day of going looking for the gorilla on purpose.

Linked skill

If you’ve done Zoom in, zoom out in the Aware toolkit, this will feel familiar. Think of it as zooming out once a day, onto the part of your day you normally miss.

How it works

Three reps.
One savoured.

Once a day, after training or before bed, you write down three things. Each one points your attention at something it tends to skip.

1
A rep I’d want to see again

Proof you can do it

Something you did today that you’d put on your highlights reel. Write it like you’re describing the clip.

2
A rep I did anyway

Nerves and all

Something you did while the doubt or the nerves were still going. Name the noise first, then what you did.

3
Something I was given

Who or what helped

Someone or something that made today a bit better, and what they actually did.

Then savour one.Pick one and replay it for a minute. Go back to where you were and how it felt in your body, and give it time to sink in before you move on.

You don’t have to tell yourself you’re great. Your mind wouldn’t buy it anyway, as you’ll know from The Documentary of You. You’re just writing down what happened, hard bits and all. Confidence built on things you’ve actually done, especially the things you did with the doubt shouting at you, lasts a lot longer than confidence built on feeling good or on what other people say.

Four rules

Rule 1
The bar is low on purpose

If it happened and it helped, write it down. “I turned up” is a rep. Set the bar too high and you’ll hardly ever see yourself succeed, and your confidence drops with it.

Rule 2
Write it like a clip

Go with “I kept calling for the ball after my first drop” over “I’m a strong player.” You can repeat an action. A label falls over the first time you have a bad day.

Rule 3
Hard days still count

The hard stuff can sit right alongside your reps. A rubbish day with one honest rep in it is a good entry.

Rule 4
No streaks

Miss a day and you haven’t lost anything. Just pick it up again tonight.

Why it works

Belief that you can do something mostly comes from seeing yourself do it. Bandura called this self efficacy, and he found your own successes build it more than anything else, including what a coach tells you. The catch is that most of those successes slip past without you noticing.

Skill learning research points the same way. In motor learning studies, people who got feedback after their better attempts learned more and felt more capable than people who got it after their worst ones. When the bar for a good attempt was set so high that people hardly ever cleared it, their confidence and their learning both dropped. So the bar here is low.

How you word it makes a difference too. Kids praised for their kicks coped better with a later setback than kids who were told they were great players, so stick to what you did.

The Sunday look back is there because comparing yourself with yourself helps. Seeing their own improvement over time helped people learn and settled their nerves.

Gratitude is in there because it’s good for you. Writing down what you’re grateful for has been linked with better wellbeing and better sleep, and more recently with mental toughness.

And it’s ten minutes because short daily practice is what athletes actually stick with.

Three Good Reps in six lines

  • Attention is a spotlight. It sees what it’s counting.
  • Go looking for the gorilla. Three reps and one savoured, ten minutes a day.
  • Write it like a clip. Say what you actually did.
  • The bar is low on purpose. “I turned up” counts.
  • The rep you did anyway usually has the most in it.
  • No streaks. Miss a day, pick it up tonight.
