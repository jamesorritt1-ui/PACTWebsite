01 · Where we started

Hold fast to
what holds you

Whakataukī

Kia mau ki te aka matua

Hold fast to the parent vine.

When Tāwhaki climbed to the heavens, his grandmother Whaitiri told him to take the aka matua, the main vine, anchored and strong. Not the aka tāepa, the loose vine that hangs free and swings with every gust. He held to what was anchored, and it held him.

We opened the night with this because it says what the whole session is about. What you paddle for is the aka matua. The tiredness, the nerves and the results are the wind. They will blow. The question is what you hold onto while they do.

The rest of the night came at that question in three ways: making room for what shows up, noticing where you are, and moving toward what matters. We call them open, aware and engaged, and you’ll see those words above each section as you go down the page.

Versions of the story of Tāwhaki differ between iwi and between tellers. This is one telling, offered lightly.

02 · Open

The harder we push,
the more it sticks

One of the first things we tried was an experiment. For 30 seconds, don’t think about a hot jam doughnut: sugar on the outside, warm jam in the middle, the smell of it from the van. For most of us, it was the first thing that turned up, and it kept coming back.

Then we tried it with something real. A cold Thursday night, you’re tired and you can’t be bothered, and you tell yourself “I’m not tired, I’m not tired.” Or nerves on the start line, and you try to talk yourself out of them. The harder we try to push a thought or a feeling away, the more it tends to stick, and the more energy the pushing takes. If we wait for tiredness or doubt to leave before we paddle, we can be waiting a long time.

1
It shows up
Tired, flat, nervous, doubting.
2
We push it away
“I’m not tired. I’m not tired.”
3
It sticks harder
Back sooner, and louder.
4
The stroke pays
The energy goes on the pushing, not the paddling.
And round again

It’s a death grip on the paddle. The harder you squeeze, the quicker your arms go.

Try the experiment again at home
  1. Set a timer for 30 seconds.
  2. Try as hard as you can not to think about a hot jam doughnut.
  3. Notice how often it turns up, and what happens the harder you try.
  4. Now think of something that usually sticks before training, like “I can’t be bothered tonight”. What happens when you argue with it? What happens when you just notice it and get your gear on anyway?
03 · Open

The green note
you wore all night

So if pushing the noise away doesn’t work, what does? That’s what the green note was for. Each of us wrote one piece of noise we’d brought in with us on a green post-it. A frustration, a worry, something left over from the day or the week. Then we stuck it on ourselves, and it stayed there for the whole session.

What it said was yours. Here are the kinds of noise people tend to bring to training:

A rough day at work
Frustrated with my shoulder
Something going on at home
Annoyed about the crew list
Too tired for this tonight
Worried about the next race

Examples only. Nothing anyone wrote on the night is on this page.

Then we got on with the session. Some of the time you’d have noticed the note. Some of the time you’d have forgotten it was there. Either way, you still talked, listened, laughed, found the words for your coin and built your crew stroke. The noise didn’t have to go anywhere for you to take part.

Getting tangled

All eyes on the note

Rereading it, arguing with it, trying to work it out or pull it off. Every bit of attention goes on the note, and there’s not much left for the session, the people or the stroke.

Wearing it

Let it ride along

It’s there. You can see it if you look down. You don’t have to like it or agree with it. And your attention goes on what you’re doing.

You don’t have to get rid of the noise to paddle. Notice it, let it ride along, and keep going.

Try it at your next training
  1. On the way to training, notice the noise you’re bringing with you today.
  2. Name it in a few words, as if you were writing it on the green note.
  3. Picture the note on your sleeve. You don’t need to fix it or argue with it.
  4. Get on with the session. Afterwards, ask yourself: how much did it actually get in the way?
04 · All three

Open, aware,
engaged

There’s a name for what you were doing with that green note: psychological flexibility. In one sentence, it means making room for whatever shows up, noticing where you are, and still doing what matters. It has three parts, and you’ll recognise all of them from the boat.

Open

Room for what shows up

The green note. Noise, nerves and tiredness come in the boat. We let them ride along instead of fighting them or getting tangled in them.

Aware

Here, in this stroke

The drum, the timing, the back in front of you. Not the last race or the finish line. A habit you already have can bring you back here, and there’s more on that further down.

Engaged

Moving toward what matters

What you paddle for, and the small actions that show it. That was the rest of the night.

A question to take on the water

Which of the three comes easiest to you in the boat? And which one goes first when the pressure is on? There is no right answer. Noticing which one slips is the start of being able to bring it back.

05 · Engaged

The finish line and
the sweep’s oar

Think about your sweep. She doesn’t move the boat. You do. But she decides where all that effort goes, and she keeps steering whether you’re leading or two lengths down.

A goal

is the finish line

You can want it, and you should. But a bad start, a cancelled regatta or a body that says “not this season” can take it off you.

A value

is the sweep’s oar

How you want to paddle. You can steer by it on your best day and your worst, even on a day you’re sitting on the bank.

Finish line or oar? Tap each one to check.

“Win the 200 at South Islands”Tap to check
Finish line. A great goal, and one the draw, the weather or a body can take away.
“Pull my weight on every stroke”Tap to check
Oar. You can do this whatever the result, and whatever seat you’re in.

Goals still matter. The question is what you steer by when the result goes against you. That’s the aka matua again: the finish line swings with the draw, the weather and the body. The oar is anchored.

07 · Engaged and open

Your coin,
both sides

On the night, we found what we paddle for by looking at an ordinary moment, not a medal. You can do this again at home:

  1. Close your eyes, or rest them on the floor.
  2. Find one small, ordinary moment in or around the boat that felt right. Maybe the boat suddenly ran and everyone was in time. Maybe a laugh on the way down to the water.
  3. Where are you sitting? What can you see and hear?
  4. Now imagine you’re standing on the bank, watching yourself in that moment. What words describe the woman in that seat?
Front · What I paddle for
In the heat of the battle, I want to be a paddler who is…
Turn it over
Back · The flip side
What shows up because I care…

Two checks for the front of your coin. If the answer to both is yes, you’ve found an oar, not a finish line.

  • Would this still matter if no one was on the bank and no times were recorded?
  • Could you paddle this way on a day you finish last?

The flip side. What we care about is usually exactly where we hurt. “I’ll let the crew down” only sticks because you care about the crew. “I’m not fit enough” sticks because you care about pulling your weight. The back of the coin holds the hard bits that come with what’s on the front. It’s often where the noise on a green note comes from.

You can’t keep one side of a coin and throw the other away.

If you want the side that says “I paddle for my crew”, the side that says “I’m scared of letting them down” comes in the boat too. That isn’t a fault. It’s what caring costs, and it’s proof that you do.

I care about ______ and I notice ______

Not but. “But” makes one side cancel the other. “And” lets both be true, and leaves you free to paddle.

The back of your coin is yours alone. No one will ever ask you to read it out or share it. Keep the coin somewhere you’ll see it.

08 · Engaged

The crew
stroke

Values only show up in what we do. Each crew designed one small behaviour the whole boat could do at training that shows a value it shares. Then we built it to stick, using four laws adapted from James Clear’s Atomic Habits.

01

Make it obvious

“When ___ happens, we ___.” A clear cue, and a keyword the crew chooses.

02

Make it attractive

Tie it to something the crew already enjoys, like the cuppa or the walk down with the boat.

03

Make it easy

Under two minutes, and doable on a cold, tired night.

04

Make it satisfying

Name It at the trailer: two paddlers name one thing they saw someone do that fits the word.

You don’t need all four. Pick the one law that fits your crew’s stroke best, depending on what’s most likely to get in the way.

The statue test. If a statue could do it, it isn’t a behaviour. A statue can “not complain”. A statue can’t say “together” at the trailer.

Fails the test

“We’ll always be positive.”

“Not complain.”

Passes the test

“When we lift the boat off the trailer, someone says ‘together’ and no one carries alone.”

09 · Engaged

My one
stroke

One small action that shows your value, taken at training. This is the left side of the Paddle Card in your pack.

You already have the habits.
Now find the why.

Most of you already have plenty of small habits around training: the way you put your gloves on, tapping the bow before you launch, the walk down with the boat, the same stretch on the dock. You don’t need to invent new ones.

What the values work in session one gave us was time to stop and explore why. When you know the why behind a habit, it stops being something you do on autopilot and becomes a choice that points at what you paddle for. The skill isn’t the habit itself. It’s taking the time to understand why you do what you do.

Habits as
anchors

A habit happens at the same moment, every time. That makes it a good anchor: a reminder, already part of your training, to come back to the present, right here in this stroke, and to what you paddle for. You don’t have to remember to be present. The habit does the remembering for you.

1 · The habit
Something you already do
No new routine. The moment is already built into your training.Tap the bow before we launch.
2 · Aware
Arrive
Use it to land in this moment: your feet, your breath, the water, the paddler in front.One breath out. Feet on the dock.
3 · Engaged
Remember why
Connect it to your value, then get on with the stroke.Steady.

The habit is the anchor. The value is the why.

Pick one law,
not all four

Same as the crew stroke, you don’t need all four laws. Pick the one that fits your stroke best, depending on what’s most likely to get in the way:

If you keep forgetting

Make it obvious

Tie it to a cue you can’t miss. A habit you already have is perfect.

If it feels like a chore

Make it attractive

Pair it with something or someone you enjoy, like the walk down or the cuppa.

If it’s too much on a bad day

Make it easy

Shrink it to the two minute version you can do on a cold, sore night.

If you do it, but it fades

Make it satisfying

Give it a finish: a tick, a nod, or a word at the trailer.

Your printed Paddle Card has a space for each law. Just fill in the one you’ve chosen.

How the anchor works on the day

Nerves on the start line? The tap brings her back to her feet, her breath and this stroke, and then to steady. A bad result? She asks: did I paddle steady? 10 · Take it to training

What to do
at training

Session one covered a lot of ideas. This is what they look like on the water. Six small tools, each one something you can actually do at your next training, whether that’s Saturday morning or a cold Thursday night. Most take under a minute, and nobody needs to know you’re using them.

None of them will make tiredness, nerves or doubt go away. That isn’t the job. They help you paddle the way you want to with those things in the boat.

If you only do one thing

Take your one stroke at training, then ask the paddler behind you how hers went.

Before you get in the boat10 seconds

The coin check

In the car park, or while you get changed.

  1. Look at your coin, or picture the word on the front of it.
  2. Say your word once, in your head.
  3. Ask: how do I want to paddle today? Pick one thing you’ll do that fits it.

“Today I paddle for my crew. I’ll be first to the boat.”

From your coin and the aka matua

Any time a thought sticksOne stroke

Name it, then paddle

On the drive down, in the third piece, on the start line.

  1. Notice the noise and give it a name: “there’s the too old note.”
  2. Don’t argue with it, and don’t stop to read it over and over.
  3. Feel your feet on the footrest and your hands on the paddle, and take the next stroke with the note still on your sleeve.

“There’s the tired note. It can ride along.”

From the jam doughnut and the green note

When it gets hard10 seconds

The ten second reset

Between pieces, or straight after the timing falls apart.

  1. Open: one long breath out, shoulders down. Let whatever is there be there.
  2. Aware: find the drum, or the back of the paddler in front.
  3. Engaged: ask what your word looks like in the next piece, and go on the next catch.

“Breathe out. Find the drum. Next catch.”

From open, aware, engaged

After a piece, a race or a bad result30 seconds

Steer by the oar

When the result goes against you, or you’re replaying a bad piece on the way home.

  1. Swap “did we win?” for “did I paddle like my word?”
  2. Find one moment where you did.
  3. Find one moment you’d like back, and what you’d do instead next time.

“We came fourth. I pulled my weight right to the line.”

Extra · A reflection for home

If your actions
had subtitles

An extra activity for a quiet half hour before session two. It takes the work on your coin one step further, and looks at your actions from the outside: what do they say to the people around you?

Nobody in the boat can hear what you value. They can only see what you do. Values aren’t something we announce. They show up in what we do, and other people read them whether we mean them to or not. Most of us can name a moment when someone’s actions said something to us without a word: a coach who stayed back after training, a crew mate who waited for us on the walk down, someone who sat with us after a hard race.

Think of the subtitles on a film. They tell you what’s being said when you can’t hear it. This activity starts with other people’s actions and the messages we take from them, then turns to yours.

If we could put subtitles under your actions in the boat, what would you want them to say?

Start here · about two minutes

Year in Search 2019

Google

Every December Google makes a short film from what the world searched for. In 2019, searches for heroes soared. Some are famous, like Eliud Kipchoge running the first marathon under two hours. Plenty are everyday people: teenagers catching a boy who fell from a ski lift, a young son hugging his dad after a loss at the French Open. It ends with a dedication: “To everyone using their powers to empower others.”

As you watchDon’t try to take it all in. Just notice which moment catches you.

Twenty to thirty minutesAfter the video. On your own, or with the paddler who sits behind you.

A pen and paperOr a few sticky notes. Your coin too, if it’s handy. You can also fill in step five on this page.

Yours to keepPick two or three questions from each step, not all of them. Share only what you want to.

1

The moment that stayed with you

When the video finishes, sit for a few seconds before you do anything. Then choose one person or moment that stayed with you. Think about what they actually did, and what you took from it.

The film moves between helping others, attempting something very hard, working together and sheer commitment. There’s no right moment to pick. What it means is up to you.

Ask yourself
  • Which moment stayed with you, and what was it about that moment?
  • What did the person actually do that mattered to you?
  • What did their action say, without anyone explaining it?
  • What do you admire about the way they went about it?
  • What might your reaction tell you about what you care about?
If your first answer is “they were amazing”, go one step further: what was it about what they did that made them amazing to me?
2

Give that moment a subtitle

Imagine you could write one line underneath that moment, capturing what it meant to you. It doesn’t have to be what the person intended. It’s the message you took from watching.

You’re worth stopping for. We can do hard things together. I’m willing to try, even if I fall short. What I do can help someone else.

These are examples of what someone might take from the film, not lines from it.

Now bring it closer to home. You’ve almost certainly had a message like this from someone in your own paddling, or your own life.

Ask yourself
  • Why does that particular message matter to you?
  • Where have you had a similar message from someone in the boat, the club or the rest of your life?
  • What did they actually do that let you feel it?
  • What difference did it make to how you went into the next stroke, the next piece or the next day?
3

Write three subtitles of your own

Now turn it around. You say something through your actions too: at training, in a race, on the walk down with the boat, and in how you treat yourself on a hard day. Think about what you’d like those actions to stand for, and write one subtitle for each.

Subtitle one

How I paddle

What do I want my actions to say about how I learn, train and race, and how I meet a hard piece?

Subtitle two

How I treat my crew

What do I want to say through how I treat my crew mates, our sweep and coach, and the other crews?

Subtitle three

How I treat myself

What do I want my actions to say when I struggle, make a mistake, or my body is having a hard day?

Ask yourself
  • What would you like the paddlers around you to feel when they’re in the boat with you?
  • What do you want to stand for when things are going badly?
  • Which of the three feels most like you?
  • Would it still matter if nobody noticed or praised it?
  • Could you live it on a day you finish last?

Then look underneath. What value sits under that subtitle? “You belong here” might be kindness or inclusion. “I’m willing to learn” might be curiosity. Check it against the front of your coin. Is it already there? If not, does it want to be?

Keep this about what you choose to do. How other people read your actions is partly out of your hands, and one hard moment doesn’t define you.
4

Ten seconds the camera would catch

Pick one subtitle and one real moment that could happen in the boat or on the dock. Picture the next ten seconds. What would a camera actually catch you doing? Keep it to one small action. If you’re doing this with a crew mate, talk it through, or walk it through on the dock.

Ask yourself
  • What makes that action an expression of your value?
  • What thoughts, feelings or urges might make it hard?
  • If those feelings stayed, what small action could you still choose?
  • How could you show the same value a different way if the moment changed?
This is open, aware, engaged in ten seconds: make room for what shows up, notice where you are, then do the small thing. Dr Russ Harris describes a version of the same move as ACE: acknowledge, connect, engage.

Need immediate support?

The Performance Act is a sport psychology practice, not a crisis or emergency service. If you or someone you care about needs immediate support, please reach out to one of these trusted services below. They are free, confidential, and available 24 / 7.

  • 1737 — Need to Talk?

    Free call or text, any time. Trained counsellors, 24/7.

    Call or text 1737

  • Youthline

    For young people. Free call or text 234, 24/7.

    0800 376 633

  • Lifeline NZ

    Crisis support and suicide prevention, 24/7.

    0800 543 354

  • Healthline

    Free health advice from trained staff, 24/7.

    0800 611 116

More resources at Mental Health Foundation NZ and depression.org.nz.
