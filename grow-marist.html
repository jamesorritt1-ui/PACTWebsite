01 · Start here

What this is, and
who it's for

Welcome to The Mind Gym. This is a self-guided space built for Marist Albion Rugby Academy by The Performance Act. It follows a live session called Meeting Your Mind — but you don't need to have been there to use it. If you missed the session, or just want a refresher, read on: each idea is explained from the ground up, and you can move through it at your own pace.

So, what are mental skills? Your skills and fitness get you to the moment. Mental skills decide what you do in it — the gap between knowing what to do at training and doing it when the game is on the line, the crowd is loud, and your head is full of noise.

The good news is that, like a pass or a tackle, these are skills — which means they can be practised. None of this is about being tougher or thinking positively. It's about a few simple, practical moves you can actually run when it matters. Over the next sections we'll build up the picture: how the mind creates "noise," a simple process called ACE for handling it, the split-second choice point where it all happens, and the habits that quietly pull us off our game.

02 · The model we work from

Signal & Noise

The noise doesn't need to stop for you to perform. Your job is to hear the signal clearly through it. You can't control what your mind throws at you — but you can always control what you do next.

The Signal — what helps you play

  • The job right in front of you
  • Your trained technique
  • The kind of player you want to be
  • The next action that matters

The Noise — the chatter that pulls you off it

  • Self-doubt and "not good enough"
  • Worry about the result
  • Comparing yourself to others
  • Fear of failing or being judged
03 · Your toolkit

Meet ACE

One simple process you can run in any tough moment — a missed tackle, selection stress, a bad call, the doubt that creeps in late in a game.

A

Acknowledge

Be open · Notice · Name · Make room

See what's happening in your mind and body — without being yanked straight into reaction. Name it, and a command turns back into just a thought.

C

Connect

Be present · Ground · Anchor · Refocus

Come back to the next useful cue when pressure or frustration pulls your attention away. Feet, breath, eyes up — anchors bring you back.

E

Engage

Do what matters · Clarify · Choose · Commit

Act in a way that reflects your role, your values, and the team's standards — even when it's uncomfortable. Do the next thing that matters.

ACE in real moments

It's easier to see ACE when it's tied to moments you actually know. Here are three — notice it's the same three moves every time.

You knock the ball on early
The crowd groans and the doubt spikes.
AAcknowledge: notice the heat in your chest and the "here we go again" thought. Name it — "that's the doubt" — so it's a thought, not an order.
CConnect: feet on the grass, one breath out, eyes up to the next breakdown.
EEngage: get to the next ruck and do your job. The next contest is the only one that matters now.
You've been named on the bench
Disappointment, and comparing yourself to others.
AAcknowledge: let the disappointment be there. It makes sense — it's there because you care.
CConnect: bring your attention back to what's actually in front of you — today's training.
EEngage: train like the player you want to be, so you're ready the moment your number's called.
A bad call goes against you
Anger, and the urge to argue or switch off.
AAcknowledge: notice the anger rising and name it — before it grabs the wheel.
CConnect: a cue word — "next" — and a breath to reset your attention.
EEngage: reset the line, talk to a teammate, do the next useful thing for the team.

Same three moves, any moment: notice what's here, come back to now, do what matters. You don't have to feel calm or confident first — ACE is something you do, not something you wait to feel.

04 · Where the skill lives

The choice point

Pressure and noise will always show up — they're not the problem, and they're not a sign that anything's wrong with you. Even the best players in the world feel them. The difference is what they do next.

The choice point is that split-second between what shows up (the nerves, the doubt, the bad call) and what you actually do. It's a tiny gap, but it's where everything happens — because in that gap, you can go one of two ways:

Pressure & noise show up
The choice point

↑ Toward

Run ACE. Do the next useful thing — even with the nerves still there. This is a move toward the player and teammate you want to be.

↓ Away

React, force it, shrink, or check out. These "away" moves have a name — the DOTS — and they're next.

You won't always pick "toward," and that's completely human — nobody does. The skill isn't being perfect; it's simply noticing the choice point more often. Once you can see the gap, you get to choose what happens in it. That's the whole game.

05 · The away moves

DOTS — how we
duck out of the noise

When the noise gets loud, we all reach for quick ways to escape it. They feel like relief in the moment — but they tend to pull you away from the game. There are four common ones, and they spell DOTS. Spotting yours is how you find your choice point.

D
Distract

Scroll, joke it off, focus on anything but the next job.

O
Opt out

Hide, stop calling for the ball, pull out of the contact.

T
Time travel

Stuck replaying the last mistake — or jumping to the result.

S
Self-sabotage

Turn on yourself, lash out, or numb it instead of facing it.

It always depends on the context

Here's the part that's easy to miss: the DOTS aren't "bad," and the goal isn't to never do them. Whether a move helps or hurts depends entirely on the context — the situation it's happening in, and whether it moves you toward or away from what matters right then.

The exact same action can be either. Scrolling your phone to switch off and relax the night before a game might be just what you need. Scrolling instead of warming up pulls you away from it. Replaying a clip to learn from a mistake is useful; replaying that mistake on a loop in the middle of the next game is "time travel" that drags you off the very next job. Same behaviour — different context, different result.

So the question is never "is this good or bad?" It's: "right here, right now, is this moving me toward the player I want to be, or away from them?" Same move, different moment, different answer. Getting good at asking that question — in the context you're actually in — is the skill.

06 · Watch & spot

How the best
get ready

One of the clearest ways to learn this is to watch athletes who've built it into a routine. In this clip, Olympic luge athletes Matt Mortensen and Jayson Terdiman talk through how they get ready in the moments before a run — when the pressure is at its highest and there's no second chance.

While you watch, see what you can spot: what do they put their attention on to get ready and reset? What do they do with their nerves? There's no need to take notes — just watch like you're studying another player's game.

What you might have spotted

Have a think first, then open this up. Notice how what they do lines up with ACE — the same framework we've been building.

A · Acknowledge — making room for the nerves

Rather than fighting the nerves, Mortensen pictures water flowing — fluid and unstoppable. He's not pretending he isn't nervous; he's making room for it and letting it move through, instead of getting tangled in an "I'm nervous" story. That's being open.

C · Connect — anchoring attention on the now

They look at the track and the start ramp to run the next moment in their mind, and use a song — J. Cole's "Crunch Time" — as a trigger to drop into focus. Their eyes, the track, the music: these are all anchors that bring their attention back to the present.

E · Engage — trusting the routine

They make the point that they don't need an exceptional day — just to execute what they've practised and trust their preparation. That's committed action: doing the job you know how to do, whether or not the nerves have settled.

Their anchors won't all be yours — water imagery and a particular song are personal. The skill to borrow is the idea: a few reliable things you can put your attention on to get ready and reset. You'll build your own in the drills.
