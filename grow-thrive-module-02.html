Acknowledge I · The first ACE move

Be Open: Acceptance

~20 minutes Read · practise · journal Private to you

By the end of this module you can

  • Define acceptance (making room) and tell it apart from resignation or "just putting up with it".
  • Run the Drop the Rope practice to stop fighting an uncomfortable feeling.
  • Name a sensation in your body instead of being run by it.
  • Connect acceptance to the A of ACE and the Be Open corner of your map — and train it all week.
The move Not giving up See it move Watch Activity: Drop the Rope Activity: Name it Reflect This week's Mind Gym

01 · The first move

Stop wrestling.
Make room.

In Module 01 you saw the trap: the mind makes noise, and fighting the noise makes it louder and pulls you off the job. So if control is a losing game, what's the alternative? It's the first move of ACE — Acknowledge — and it starts with a skill called acceptance.

Acceptance is willingly making room for a difficult feeling instead of fighting it. When the nerves, the doubt or the frustration show up, you stop trying to shove them down or wish them away. You let them be there — and you get on with what matters anyway. This is the "A" in ACE, and the Be Open corner of your map.

"You don't have to like the feeling. You don't have to want it. You just have to be willing to have it — so it stops running the show."

— The heart of acceptance

Here's the counter-intuitive part: making room for a feeling is what drains its power. Not because you've defeated it, but because you've stopped feeding it energy. The feeling can be there, and you can still throw the pass, sit the exam, or have the hard conversation. That's the whole skill.

02 · A crucial distinction

Acceptance is not
giving up

This is the misunderstanding that trips everyone up, so let's clear it up now. Acceptance is not resignation, giving in, or gritting your teeth and suffering. It's not saying "I feel terrible and there's nothing I can do." It's an active, deliberate choice to lean in to discomfort so you can take thoughtful action — the exact opposite of collapse.

Acceptance is…
  • Actively making room for a feeling
  • A choice: "I'm willing to have this"
  • Aimed at freeing you to act on what matters
  • Curious and kind towards what shows up
  • Something you do while moving forward
Acceptance is not…
  • Resignation or "putting up with it"
  • Liking, wanting or approving of the feeling
  • Giving up on the goal or the effort
  • Suppressing, numbing or gritting teeth
  • Passive — it's the opposite of collapse

Think of a surfer. They can't stop the waves — but they don't fight the ocean, and they don't give up and drown either. They stop struggling against the water so they can do the thing that matters: ride. Acceptance is you, stopping the fight with the water, so you can play.

03 · See it move

The tug-of-war
with the monster

Here's the whole idea in one moving picture — and you're driving it. Drag the slider to pull harder against the anxiety-monster and watch the Suffering meter climb. Then hit Drop the rope and see what changes… and what doesn't.

Suffering

Watch the one thing that never changes: the size of the monster. Struggling doesn't shrink the feeling — it just piles on suffering. Letting go doesn't delete it either — it frees your hands.

That's acceptance, in one image. The monster — your anxiety, doubt, frustration — doesn't get smaller when you fight it, and it doesn't vanish when you let go. But the instant you drop the rope, all the energy you were pouring into the struggle is suddenly free: for the pass, the exam, the conversation. Now let's turn that into something you can actually do.

Watch · see it done

Dropping the rope,
on screen

You’ve driven the tug-of-war yourself — now watch the metaphor, then a champion living it. Both play right here on the page.

Catch these moments · 45 minutes before the start

“I tell myself it’s just skiing, and it’s just shooting. I know how to do both of those things well.” — his mind wants to make the Olympics enormous. He hands it something ordinary and true instead.

He invites the feelings in early. That’s making room in advance: if you’re expecting the monster, there’s no rope to grab.

1:19

“Do what you can, with what you have, where you are… a song comes on that distracts you for a second… that reset button is just repeating that quote.” — when a distraction hooks him, there’s no fight and no frustration — one repeated line brings him back. A reset, not a battle.

1:37

“I look at the snow. I look at where I am. I’ve got skis on my feet. I’ve got a rifle on my back.” — grounding, in plain sight: what he can see, what he can feel, exactly where he is. You’ll build this exact move in Module 04.

1:54

“I’m the bow with the arrow drawn, waiting… when that beep changes, that’s the release — and I’m the arrow.” — all that tension in his body? He doesn’t fight it down. He loads it. The drawn bow is the nerves, accepted and pointed at the race.

Notice what he never does in the whole film: he never tries to feel calm. He plans for the pressure, names what he’ll feel before it arrives, grounds in what’s real, and turns the tension into a drawn bow. That’s dropping the rope — 45 minutes before an Olympic race.

04 · Your practice

Activity: Drop the Rope

You just felt it in the picture above — now do it for real, with a feeling of your own. As long as you pull, you're locked in a struggle you can't win. But there's a third option that isn't winning or losing: drop the rope. This short guided practice walks you through it. Find a quiet-ish minute, bring to mind a feeling you usually try to get rid of, and step through it.

Guided practice · ~3 minutes
Drop the Rope
Step 1 of 6

Settle

Breathe

Sit or stand tall. Feet flat on the floor. Let your eyes soften or gently close. Take one slow breath — and make the out-breath longer than the in-breath.

However that felt is fine — there's no "doing it right". If the feeling was still there at the end, that's success, not failure. The goal was never to get rid of it. The goal was to stop fighting it and still choose your next move.

Want to just breathe for a moment? Swipe slowly through the paced breath below — let each cue land before you move to the next.

Swipe → slowly
Settle

Sit or stand tall. Feet flat. Let your eyes soften.

Breathe in

Slow, through the nose — about four counts.

Tiny pause

Rest at the top for a moment. No strain.

Longer out

Breathe out slowly — about six counts. Longer than the in-breath.

Again ×3

Keep going — out-breath always a little longer than the in.

Come back

Feel your feet. The feeling can ride along. Choose your next move.

05 · A 20-second skill

Activity: Name it to tame it

Feelings live in the body — a tight chest, a fluttery stomach, a buzzing in the hands. One of the simplest acceptance skills is just to notice where it is and give it a name. Research on "affect labelling" suggests that quietly naming a feeling can take some of the charge out of it — and more importantly, it tells you exactly what you're working with. Build the sentence below — it's the exact move you'll run in real time.

1 · Where do you feel it?
2 · What does it feel like?

Right now, I notice something … in my …

That's the whole move. You're not fixing it or fighting it — you're making room for it by naming it. "Here's tension." "Here's anxiety." Then you carry on.

06 · Reflect

Make it yours

A couple of honest lines. Answer these in your notebook — one honest line each is plenty.

In your notebook

Which feeling do you most often try to get rid of before you perform?

Nerves, doubt, anger, flatness, pressure in the chest — name the specific one.

In your notebook

After Drop the Rope — what changed, and what didn't?

Often the feeling stays similar, but the struggle with it drops. What did you notice?

In your notebook

In your own words — what's the difference between acceptance and giving up?

This is your retrieval question. Say it how you'd explain it to a teammate.

Apply in Sport

Before your next session, name the feeling you usually try to get rid of — then practise making room for it for 60 seconds instead. Notice you can hold it and warm up well.

Apply in Life

Homesickness, social anxiety, exam dread, the sting of being dropped or injured — pick one uncomfortable feeling outside sport this week and practise dropping the rope. The same willingness that lets you compete also lets you live.

07 · This week's Mind Gym

The Make Room
7-day journal

Reading about acceptance changes nothing — reps do. This is your training for the week: one entry a day for seven days. Each day, once, when a difficult feeling shows up, run the move — name it, make room, drop the rope — then log it here in under a minute. Watch what happens to the struggle across the week.

How to use it: tap a day to open it. Jot what showed up, where you felt it, and whether you made room or fought it — no judgement either way, honesty is the training. Tick the day off when you've logged it. Keep the streak in your notebook — one line a day is the whole job. (The tracker below is just a helper for this browser.)

0 / 7 days

Your "Be Open" pocket card

  1. Notice the feeling you'd normally push away.
  2. Name it — "Here's tension," "Here's anxiety." Where is it in your body?
  3. Make room — breathe around it; let it be there.
  4. Drop the rope — stop the tug-of-war.
  5. Choose the one useful thing to do next — and do it.

Lock it in

Talk it over

Take one of these to a mate, your team, or your coach this week. Saying it out loud is where it gets real.

  • A teammate says: “So acceptance means I should just stop caring?” How would you put them right in one sentence?
  • What feeling is quietly “not allowed” in your changing room — and what does hiding it cost people?
  • Where’s the line between making room for discomfort and pushing through something you shouldn’t (like an injury)? Hint: ask which one moves you towards the athlete you want to be.

The rep for this week: once a day, make room for a feeling you'd normally fight, and log it. Acceptance is half of Being Open — it handles the feelings. Next module tackles the other half: defusion — unhooking from the sticky thoughts that try to grab the wheel.
