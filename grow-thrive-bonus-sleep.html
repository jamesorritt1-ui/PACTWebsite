Why it matters

Sleep is your
hidden superpower

In Module 00 we flagged sleep as the big one. Here’s the deep dive. If a supplement did for you what a good night’s sleep does, it would be banned as a performance enhancer. Sleep quality and quantity is widely regarded as an athlete’s single best recovery strategy — and skimping on it quietly taxes everything you’re training for.1

A clear boundary first. The Performance Act is a sport-psychology practice — not a sleep clinic. Everything here is general, evidence-based education, not personalised medical advice, and it doesn’t replace a doctor. If sleep is a persistent problem, please see the professionals flagged near the end.

While you sleep, your brain and body do their most important repair and consolidation work — and it happens in specific stages:

Deep sleep repairs you

In deep (slow-wave) sleep your body releases most of its growth hormone, repairing muscle and tissue damaged in training. Miss it and recovery and adaptation suffer.2

It locks in skills

Slow-wave sleep consolidates the motor skills and memories you practised that day — sleep is when today’s reps actually “stick.”2

It resets your emotions

REM (dream) sleep processes the day’s stress and emotion in a calmer chemical state — part of why everything feels more manageable after a good night.2

It cleans the brain

Overnight the brain’s glymphatic system flushes out metabolic waste that builds up while you’re awake — a nightly deep-clean for your mind.2

Flip it around and the cost is stark: too little sleep slows reaction time, blunts decisions and accuracy, dampens mood, weakens the immune and hormone systems, and raises injury risk. And it makes the mental game harder — a tired brain finds it far more difficult to stay open, present and engaged. Worth knowing: around 63% of elite athletes report disturbed sleep the night before a big competition, and heavy training days tend to reduce sleep the following night — exactly when you need it most.3

The number

How much do you
actually need?

More than you’re probably getting. These are the mainstream, guideline-backed ranges — and hard-training athletes generally sit at the top of them, or a little beyond.

Teens · 13–18
8–10 hrs
The American Academy of Sleep Medicine’s recommendation for optimal health.4
Young adults · 18–25
7–9 hrs
And more than 9 can be appropriate when training hard or paying back sleep debt.4
Hard-training athletes
9–10+ hrs
Many sleep-in-sport researchers suggest aiming higher; some elite performers bank 10–12.4

Now the reality gap: studies find adolescent athletes average around 7 hours a night and miss the recommended 8+ on most nights — and student-athletes often sleep roughly 2 hours less than non-athletes. That’s a big, fixable edge sitting on the table.4

"When athletes deliberately extend their sleep towards 10 hours in bed, sprint times, shooting accuracy, reaction time and mood measurably improve. Sleep isn’t lost time — it’s training."

— On sleep-extension research in athletes5

Beyond hours

What good sleep
actually looks like

Sleep health is more than a number of hours. Sleep researchers describe it across several dimensions — a handy way to spot which part of your sleep needs work. (Researchers sometimes call these RU-SATED.)6

R
Regularity
Similar bed & wake times day to day.
S
Satisfaction
Sleep feels restful and good.
A
Alertness
You can stay awake & focused by day.
T
Timing
Asleep at hours that suit your life.
E
Efficiency
Fall asleep & stay asleep easily (>~85% of time in bed).
D
Duration
Enough hours for your age & load.

Notice the first one: regularity. A consistent sleep–wake rhythm — even on weekends — may matter as much as the raw hours. It’s the single habit most sleep experts start with.6

The evidence-based toolkit

The habits that
actually work

This is “sleep hygiene” — the set of habits with a solid research base for helping you sleep well.7 Tap each group to open it. You don’t need to do all of them — start with one or two.

  • Get regular. Go to bed and get up at roughly the same time every day — including weekends. This is the highest-value habit there is.
  • Build a wind-down ritual. 30–60 minutes of the same calming things each night (dim lights, stretch, breathe, read) tells your body sleep is coming.
  • Get morning daylight. Light early in the day anchors your body clock — a morning walk is a great reset.
  • Keep your daytime routine after a bad night. Don’t cancel your day because you’re tired; that only feeds the problem.
  • Dim the lights and screens 30–60 min before bed. Bright and blue light in the evening delays your melatonin (your “time-to-sleep” signal).
  • Keep the phone out of the bed. If you use it as an alarm, put it across the room.
  • Make the room dark. Blackout curtains or an eye-mask block early-morning light that wakes you too soon.
  • Cut caffeine 4–6+ hours before bed. It’s a stimulant that lingers for hours — that afternoon energy drink can still be in your system at midnight.
  • Go easy on alcohol. It might feel relaxing, but it fragments and lowers the quality of your sleep — avoid it in the hours before bed.
  • Avoid nicotine near bedtime — also a stimulant.
  • Get the food timing right. Don’t go to bed starving or stuffed — a light snack is fine; a heavy meal late can disrupt sleep.
  • Cool, dark and quiet. A slightly cool room is ideal — your core temperature naturally drops as you fall asleep.
  • A warm shower or bath 1–2 hours before bed can help: the after-drop in body temperature nudges you towards sleep.
  • Block noise with earplugs or a fan if your environment is loud.
  • Bed is for sleeping (and sex) — not scrolling, studying or gaming, so your brain links bed with sleep.
  • Train regularly — consistent exercise improves how quickly you fall asleep and how deeply you sleep.
  • Ease off the hardest sessions in the last few hours before bed where you can — intense late training can leave you too wired to settle.
  • Plan around night games and travel. Late competition and jet lag shift your body clock; a wind-down still matters even when you get home buzzing.
  • A short nap of 20–30 minutes boosts alertness, mood and recovery without leaving you groggy — great on a heavy day.
  • Avoid the 30–75 minute zone — you often wake mid-deep-sleep feeling worse (“sleep inertia”).
  • If you have time, a full ~90-minute nap completes a sleep cycle and can aid memory and motor learning.
  • Nap earlier in the day (before mid-afternoon) so it doesn’t steal from tonight. If you struggle to sleep at night, skip naps altogether.

Nap dosing per applied sleep-in-sport guidance.5

The ACT angle

Stop fighting
to fall asleep

Here’s where THRIVE’s skills matter most. Sleep is one thing you genuinely cannot force — and trying harder makes it worse. The 2am spiral (“I have to sleep, I’ve got training, why won’t my brain switch off”) is the struggle switch from Module 01, flipped fully on. The effort is the problem.

So two evidence-based moves that also happen to be pure ACT. First, stimulus control: if you’re not asleep after about 20 minutes, don’t lie there wrestling — get up, do something calm and boring in dim light, and return to bed only when sleepy. It stops your bed becoming a place of struggle.7 Second, drop the rope with sleep — run ACE for the racing mind:

A
AcknowledgeNotice the racing thoughts and the frustration without fighting them. “Here’s my mind, busy again.” You don’t have to win the argument — and you can even let go of needing to sleep. Rest, on its own, is still valuable.
C
ConnectCome back to your body. Feel the weight of the bed, and slow the breath — a long, gentle out-breath (in for 4, out for 6). You’re not breathing to force sleep; you’re grounding, and letting sleep come to you.
E
EngageTurn gently towards rest: unhook from the thoughts (“thanks, mind”), and let attention rest on the breath or the sensations of lying down. If you’re still wide awake after a while, get up — without drama — and try again later.

The paradox is real and well known to sleep scientists: the moment you stop trying to sleep and simply allow rest, sleep has room to arrive. Willingness, not effort.

Track it

Your 7-day sleep log

A short sleep diary beats guessing — it shows you the real pattern behind how you feel.7 Fill it in for a week. Keep it light (no clock-watching in the night); rough times are fine. Everything saves on this device only.

One quick entry each morning for seven days. Roughly what time you went to bed and woke, and how rested you feel. Watch what lines up with your best mornings.

Your sleep pocket card

  1. Regular beats everything — same bed & wake time, even on weekends.
  2. Aim for 8+ hours (towards 9–10 when training hard).
  3. Wind down: dim light, no screens, cool dark room, no caffeine late.
  4. Don’t fight it — if you can’t sleep, get up; run ACE; let rest come.

Know your limits

When to see a pro

Good sleep hygiene helps most people most of the time — but it isn’t a treatment for a sleep disorder. Please talk to a professional if:

Worth a conversation with your GP or a sleep specialist

  • You’ve had trouble falling or staying asleep most nights for more than about 3–4 weeks, despite good habits.
  • You snore loudly, gasp or stop breathing in the night (a partner may notice) — possible sleep apnea.
  • You’re exhausted in the day no matter how long you sleep.
  • Sleep problems are hitting your mood, anxiety or wellbeing — talk to someone (and the Immediate Support button is on every page).

Good to know: for ongoing insomnia, the recommended first-line treatment is CBT-I (cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia) — it works better in the long run than sleeping pills, which can lose effect and become habit-forming.7 Ask your GP.
