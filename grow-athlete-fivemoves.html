THE FIVE
MOVES

Notice. Name. Let go. Soften. Expand. Five things your mind has to do, in order, to get from “something is wrong” to “here’s what I’m doing about it” — in about five minutes. This is the sequence every other tool in this area is a piece of.

Why this is the star tool

One sequence.
All three pillars.

Everything else in this area is a single skill you pull out when you need it. This is different. This is the order the skills go in — and once you have it, you stop hunting through a toolkit mid-crisis and just run the sequence.

Here is the neat part, and it is not a coincidence. The five moves come from the work of Kirk Strosahl and colleagues on present-moment awareness. Strosahl is also the person who grouped the six ACT processes into the three pillars this whole area is built on — Open, Aware, Engaged. So the five moves and the TriFlex are two views of the same thing, by the same author. They line up exactly:

Why the order matters

The moves are progressive. Finishing one is what makes the next one possible. You can’t name what you haven’t noticed. You can’t let go of something you can’t name. You can’t soften around a thing you’re still fused with. And expanding — turning the whole experience into fuel for something that matters — only lands if the first four have happened.

Which is why skipping straight to the end never works. Most athletes try to jump from “I feel terrible” to “right, get on with it” and wonder why it doesn’t hold. They’ve attempted move five without doing one to four.

It will not be tidy. Expect to slip backwards. You’ll be halfway through letting go and find yourself back at square one because something new showed up. That’s not failing the exercise — that’s the exercise. The human mind is genuinely poor at staying in the present moment, which is exactly why this is trainable.

01

Notice

Showing up. Becoming aware of what’s happening, inside and out.

The move

Turn towards what’s there instead of away from it. That’s the whole move, and it’s the hardest one to do honestly, because everybody has a well-practised set of escape routines that fire the moment the heat comes up.

The escape routines are worth knowing by name: going numb, getting busy, changing the subject, going analytical, making a joke, picking a fight. Every one of them works, briefly. Every one of them costs you the information you needed.

Three levels of noticing

Think of trying to understand what a rollercoaster is like.

  1. Participant. You’re on the ride, screaming. Totally immersed — and you can’t report anything useful, because you are the experience. This is where most athletes are during a bad patch.
  2. Participant-observer. You’re on the ride and tracking what’s happening: stomach drops here, hands grip there. Both at once. This is the working level.
  3. Observer. You’re watching the ride from the ground. Maximum perspective, and useful when things are overwhelming — but stay here too long and you’ve just found a more sophisticated way to check out.

The skill isn’t picking one. It’s being able to move between them depending on what the moment needs.

Three things attention does

Orienting — pointing at something. Focusing — holding it there. Shifting — moving it when the situation changes. All three are trainable, and all three are separate. Plenty of athletes can focus like a laser and cannot shift to save their lives; that’s the player still fuming about a decision three plays later.

Why it matters — the thing nobody tells you

Feelings are not obstacles. The Latin root of “emotion” means to move — they’re signals designed to move you towards more useful behaviour.

Which means an athlete who has learned to not-feel has switched off their own guidance system. Their behaviour goes rigid and rule-driven, because the live information that would have made it flexible isn’t reaching them any more. Checking out isn’t neutral. It costs you the data.

Activity · 3 minutes

Name your escape routine

When the heat comes up, how do you disappear?

Tap all that are true. Most people have two or three favourites.

Which level do you live at — participant, participant-observer, or observer?

And which of the three attention jobs is weakest for you?

02

Name

Answering the oldest question there is: what is that?

The move

Put words on it. Specific ones.

There’s a real paradox here worth understanding. Most of this area is about loosening the grip of language — and now we’re asking you to use language deliberately. That’s not a contradiction. Sensations, memories and emotions don’t arrive in words; they arrive as a bundle, undifferentiated and often overwhelming. Words are the only tool you have for taking the bundle apart.

Three levels of naming
  1. Right category. Using an anger word when it’s anger. Sounds trivial; isn’t. Plenty of people report “stressed” for everything from grief to excitement.
  2. Telling apart two feelings in the same moment. You can be angry and hurt in the same conversation, and they need completely different responses. If it all comes out as “angry”, you’ll respond to the wrong one.
  3. Getting the intensity right. Irritated is not livid. Nervous is not terrified. Athletes who only have the top of the scale end up treating every 3 as a 9.
Why it works

People who can verbally discriminate between different internal experiences score higher on emotional regulation — the ability to read a signal and adjust behaviour rather than be driven by it. Naming is what converts a bundle into information you can use.

And when someone first turns towards an avoided experience, they usually can’t name much at all: “I don’t know, it’s just a lot.” Fifteen minutes later, with prompting, the same person can describe several distinct sensations in different places. The vocabulary isn’t fixed. It’s trainable, and it grows fast.

Activity · 4 minutes

Take the bundle apart

Bring to mind a recent moment that felt like “a lot”. Now separate it.

Name at least two different feelings that were both present

Not one feeling described twice. Two genuinely different ones — angry and ashamed, relieved and guilty, nervous and excited.

03

Let Go

Loosening your grip on the commentary — without arguing with it.

The move

Your mind never shuts up. Call it busy mind. It is superb at some jobs — crossing a road, reading a defence, planning a season — and catastrophically bad at others. The problem is that it has no built-in feature that stops it giving you advice on subjects it knows nothing about.

Busy mind’s favourite topic is judging: good/bad, right/wrong, fair/unfair, whose fault. And it loves projecting those judgements backwards and forwards in time, which is how a single dropped ball becomes a verdict on your entire career.

Letting go is not silencing it, winning against it, or agreeing with it. It’s moving your attention from the content of the commentary to the fact that commentary is happening.

Attachment, in one image

The root of “attachment” means bound to. The root of “detachment” means set apart from.

When you’re attached to an experience, the gap between you and it disappears — you’re back at the participant level from move one, being led around by whatever your mind produces next. The bits that grab hardest are always the emotionally loaded ones: harsh self-judgements, painful memories, catastrophic pictures of what’s coming.

You are not trying to live permanently detached. You’re trying to move freely between being in it and standing beside it.

Letting go is not letting it win, and it isn’t not caring. Athletes hear “detach” and think it means going flat — lowering the stakes so nothing hurts. That’s a different thing entirely, and it wrecks performance. You can be completely gripped by wanting to win and completely ungripped by your mind’s running commentary about whether you deserve to.

Activity · 4 minutes

Catch busy mind at work

What is busy mind’s favourite subject when it comes to you?

Where does it project — backwards or forwards?

Your line for stepping back from it

Something that notices the process rather than debating the content. “There’s the judging machine, going again.”

04

Soften

The move almost everyone skips — and the one that makes the rest hold.

The move

Some things won’t detach. You can name them, you can step back from the commentary, and they still stick to you like glue.

Call these your scars. Things you did that you’re not proud of: the shortcut, the lie about the injury, the way you spoke to someone after a loss. And things done to you: the coach who broke something in you at sixteen, the injury that took a year, being dropped without a conversation.

You know you’re near one when the memory makes you physically wince.

Scars don’t detach because the pain in them is a direct measure of what you care about. Detaching from that would mean detaching from the caring. So there’s a different move needed: not stepping back from it, but holding it gently.

Why you’re bad at this — and it isn’t your fault

Almost everyone is far better at compassion for other people than for themselves, and there’s a straightforward reason. From the time you learn to speak you’re rewarded for being kind to others — and corrected, told off, or punished for your own failures of self-control. Over years, you internalise that: you stop needing anyone else to deliver the criticism because you’ve learned to do it yourself, symbolically, on demand.

So your relationship with self-directed correction is negative by training, and your relationship with other-directed kindness is positive by training. Asking yourself “why can’t I be as kind to me as I am to my teammates?” won’t fix it, because it was never an intellectual problem. It’s a conditioning problem, and it takes reps, not insight.

The bit that makes this more than self-care

Softening isn’t only about feeling better. Done properly it’s a kind of judo: you accept yourself, scars included, and then use the experience of having flaws to be genuinely decent about other people’s.

Which is why the athletes who can do this are so often the ones the group trusts. Someone who has made peace with their own worst day can sit with a teammate on theirs without flinching, fixing, or making it about themselves. You cannot offer anyone else something you refuse to offer yourself.

05

Expand

The ribbon cutting. Turning all of that into something you actually do.

The move

Everything up to here has been clearing ground. This is the move where it turns into behaviour — and if it doesn’t, the whole sequence was an interesting five minutes that changed nothing.

Think of it as a ribbon cutting. You’ve built something over the previous four moves; now you put it into service. There’s often a noticeably light, almost playful quality to this bit — people are usually a little surprised by how free they feel once they’ve stopped wrestling with their own head.

The specific shift

Two things change, and they’re worth stating precisely.

In your head: from “the goal is to feel less of this” to “I’ll carry this, because of what it’s for.”

In your behaviour: from moves governed by escape and avoidance, to moves governed by what you actually value.

The pain doesn’t get removed. It gets re-read — from something traumatic that happened to you, into a reflection of something you care about deeply enough to hurt over. That reframe is what dignifies the discomfort and makes it purposeful, and it’s the difference between suffering and paying a price on purpose.

Make it small and make it now. The temptation at this point is a grand declaration — a new attitude, a fresh start on Monday. Don’t. The measure of a good expand is one specific thing you will do in the next 24 hours. Grand declarations are what busy mind offers you to avoid doing anything today.

The five moves, in five lines

  • Notice — turn towards it. Your escape routines are automatic; catching them is the skill.
  • Name — precise words. Two feelings, right intensity, and whatever is underneath.
  • Let go — watch the commentary happening instead of debating what it says.
  • Soften — hold the scar gently. It hurts because you care, and that’s information.
  • Expand — one small action in 24 hours, or the previous four were a nice sit-down.
