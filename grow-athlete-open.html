Before you start

The problem isn’t
the feeling. It’s the fight.

Your brain is very good at solving problems in the outside world. Locked out of the house? Break a window. Don’t like the peeling paint? Scrape it and repaint.

The trouble starts when you turn that same machinery inward. Try to scrape away a thought and it gets stickier. Try to force a feeling out and it gets louder. This is one of the most reliably replicated findings in psychology: deliberate attempts to suppress a thought increase how often it shows up, and how much power it has over what you do.

Which leaves you with a real problem, because the pre-match nerves, the flash of anger at a bad call, the doubt that arrives at 3—0 down — none of it is going anywhere. Nobody has ever found the delete button. There isn’t one.

Open is the alternative. Not liking the feeling. Not wanting it. Just stopping the war with it, so that all the energy currently going into the fight is available for the thing you actually came here to do.

Why this pillar exists. In the ACT model, the two processes that most narrow what you’re capable of are experiential avoidance (trying to control or get rid of what you feel) and cognitive fusion (getting so caught in the content of a thought that it dominates everything else). Together they form the pillar of inflexibility called Closed. Openness — acceptance plus defusion — is the direct antidote. Walser & Westrup, Learning ACT (2019); Hayes et al. (2012).

01

The Struggle Switch

For: when a feeling seems to be getting worse the harder you try to sort it out.

What it is

Imagine there’s a switch at the back of your mind. When it’s on, you fight every uncomfortable feeling that turns up. When it’s off, feelings are free to come and go without you going to war with them.

Watch what the switch does. Nerves show up before a game. Switch on: “Oh no, not this again. Why am I like this? I hate feeling like this. It’s going to wreck my performance.” Now you’ve got anxiety about your anxiety. Then frustration about the anxiety. Then a bit of shame about the frustration. The original nerves haven’t changed at all — but you’re now carrying four things instead of one.

Switch off: nerves show up, you notice them, you let them be there, and you go and warm up. Same nerves. A quarter of the load.

Why it works

The struggle switch is an emotion amplifier. Every layer you add is a second-order emotion — a feeling about a feeling — and each one is generated by the fight, not by the original event. Turn the switch off and the layers stop being manufactured. Your emotions are then free to rise and fall as the situation actually warrants.

It also frees up capacity. Struggling is expensive: it consumes attention, working memory and physical energy you would otherwise be putting into the task.

Worked example · a netballer, switch on

Mia gets tight hands in the last quarter of close games. She hates it, so before every close game she scans her hands to check whether the tightness has arrived. Checking for it is the struggle. Because she’s scanning, she notices every tiny change, which reads as the tightness starting, which produces a spike of dread, which produces genuine tightness. She has built a machine that reliably manufactures the exact thing she’s trying to avoid.

Switch off: Mia stops running the check. When tightness turns up she notes it — “there it is, tight hands, last quarter” — and puts her attention on the next pass. The tightness still comes some nights. It just no longer arrives with an entourage.

The trap to avoid. Turning the struggle switch off is not a clever new way to make the feeling go away. If you do it hoping the nerves will vanish, you’ve just switched the struggle back on wearing a disguise. The aim is to stop paying the extra tax — not to get rid of the original feeling.

02

Making Room — TAME It

For: a feeling that’s big and physical — the chest, the stomach, the throat — and won’t be talked out of you.

What it is

If the struggle switch is the idea, TAME is the method. Four steps for opening up around a difficult feeling and letting it be there without either obeying it or fighting it.

The four steps
  1. T — Take note. Find the feeling in your body. Where is it most? Scan head to toe. Name what you find: “here’s tightness across my chest”, “here’s a churn in my stomach”.
  2. A — Allow. Give it permission to be there. Not because you like it. Because it’s already there and arguing with reality is a losing game. Silently: “let it be”.
  3. M — Make room. Breathe into and around it. Imagine space opening up so the feeling has somewhere to sit. Then get a sense of it as an object — if it had a size, a weight, a temperature, a colour, what would they be? You are not visualising for the sake of it; describing a feeling in physical terms is what turns it from a threat into a thing you’re observing.
  4. E — Expand awareness. Keep the feeling in view and widen out. Notice the room, the sounds, your feet on the floor. The feeling is now one thing in a big picture rather than the whole picture.
Why it works

Two mechanisms. First, naming: putting an internal experience into words engages the prefrontal cortex and reliably reduces the reactivity of the brain’s threat system — the effect is well documented as “affect labelling”. Second, exposure without avoidance: staying in contact with a feeling while not running your usual escape routine teaches your nervous system that the feeling itself is survivable. It doesn’t need to be dangerous — and repeated experience is the only thing that ever really convinces a body of that.

Start small. Nobody teaches a sailor to handle a storm by sending them into one. Practise TAME first on a 3-out-of-10 feeling — mild irritation, low-level nerves, the twinge of a small disappointment. Build up. If you go straight for the biggest thing you carry, you will conclude that the skill doesn’t work, when in fact you just skipped the training.

Worked example · a swimmer, behind the blocks

T: “Stomach. High up, just under the ribs. Cold and fizzy.”
A: “Okay. You can be there. I’m not sending you away.”
M: Two slow breaths into it. “About the size of a fist. Metallic. Vibrating.” Space around it.
E: Feet on the tiles. Smell of chlorine. The starter’s voice. The block in front of her.

Total time: about forty seconds. The fizz did not go. She swam a season best. Those two facts are not in conflict — that’s the entire point.

03

Unhooking From Thoughts

For: the sentence in your head that keeps deciding what you do.

What it is

“Fusion” comes from a Latin root meaning to pour. When you’re fused with a thought, the thought and the world it describes have been poured together into one thing — like water, sugar and lemons becoming lemonade. You are no longer looking at a thought. You’re looking from it.

Think of it as sunglasses you’ve forgotten you’re wearing. Everything is tinted and you have no idea, because you can’t see the lens — only the world through it. Defusion is taking the glasses off and holding them up. Same lens. But now it’s an object you’re inspecting, not the truth about the world.

Hooked

  • “I’m going to choke” is a forecast
  • You argue with it, or obey it
  • It decides whether you take the shot
  • Your attention is inside the sentence
  • It feels like reality

Unhooked

  • “I’m going to choke” is a sentence
  • You notice it and carry on
  • You decide whether you take the shot
  • Your attention is on the ball
  • It feels like weather
Why it works

Here’s the counter-intuitive part, and it’s the single biggest thing that separates ACT from the version of “mental skills” you may have been taught before: you do not have to change the content of a thought to change its effect on you.

Trying to argue a thought down usually expands the network around it and makes it more central. Defusion sidesteps that entirely. It changes the context in which the thought occurs — and it’s context, not content, that determines whether a thought grabs the wheel. The typical result is a drop in how believable and how sticky the thought feels, without any change in how often it turns up.

The six moves

Different ones land for different people. Learn all six; keep the two that work for you.

  1. Name the thought. Put “I’m having the thought that…” in front of it. Five words that turn you from the person inside the thought into the person noticing it. Want more distance? “I notice I’m having the thought that…”
  2. Thank your mind. Your mind produces warnings because that is its job — it is a threat-detection machine that has kept your species alive. Say, warmly and without sarcasm, “Thanks, mind.” No argument starts, so nothing feeds it.
  3. Name the story. Your stickiest thoughts aren’t new — they’re reruns. Title it like a film: the Not-Good-Enough story, the Everyone’s-Watching story. When it starts playing, just note the title.
  4. Silly voice. Say it in a cartoon voice, or sing it to Happy Birthday. Identical words, different packaging. If a thought can make you smirk, you’ve just proved it’s sound, not truth.
  5. Play with the text. Picture the words on a screen. Space them out. Run them together. Change the colour, the font, make them bounce. Watch the grip loosen as the packaging changes.
  6. Repeat it fast. Say a two-word version out loud, over and over, for thirty seconds. It dissolves into noise. That noise is what it always was.

Use the right move for the weight of the thought. The silly-voice and fast-repeat moves are for everyday sticky thoughts. If a thought is tied to something genuinely painful — grief, a serious injury, something someone did to you — use moves 1 to 3. Defusion is never about mocking what actually hurts.

Helpful vs True

For: when you’re stuck arguing with your own head about whether a thought is fair.

What it is

A short-cut out of an argument you cannot win. When a harsh thought turns up — “I’m not good enough for this level” — the instinct is to check whether it’s true. Gather the evidence. List your achievements. Debate it.

You can burn hours in there, and most of the time it changes nothing, because a lot of harsh thoughts contain a grain of truth. That’s exactly what makes them stick.

So we ask a different question. Not is this true? but is this useful? If a thought helps you be the person you want to be, do what you want to do, and build the life you want — take it, use it. If it doesn’t, you don’t have to disprove it. You just don’t have to obey it.

Worked example · the client who was right

A man came in convinced he was overweight. He was. He wanted to argue about whether the thought was accurate. His clinician wouldn’t take the bet, and asked instead: “When your mind calls you lazy and disgusting — what happens next?”

“I get low.” And then? “I eat rubbish.”

So the thought was accurate and it was reliably driving the opposite of what he cared about. Both things at once. Truth turned out to be the wrong question entirely. (Adapted from Harris, The Happiness Trap, ch. 7.)

Why it works

This is workability, the pragmatic test at the centre of ACT. Instead of measuring a thought against reality, you measure it against your own direction of travel. It sidesteps the debate entirely, and it puts the decision back with you rather than with your mind.

There’s a second reason, and it matters for athletes especially. Harsh self-criticism does sometimes kick you into action — which is why so many high performers swear by it. But the long-run bill is steep: stress, burnout, exhaustion, and a self-worth that collapses the moment performance dips. This is the engine of unhealthy perfectionism. There are better motivators, and the Engaged toolkit is full of them.

Urge Surfing

For: the urge to snap at the ref, to skip the session, to check the phone, to say the thing you’ll regret.

What it is

Sit on a beach and watch a wave. It starts small, builds, gathers speed, reaches a crest — then subsides. Give it room and it does that harmlessly. Put something solid in its way and it explodes.

Urges do exactly the same thing. The instinct is to obey them or to fight them. Surfing is the third option: you neither give in nor resist. You watch the urge rise, crest and fall, and you don’t act on it.

Why it works

Most urges run their full wave in around three minutes. People often protest that theirs last for hours — and they’re right, because resisting is what stretches them out. Fighting an urge, ruminating about it, or trying to distract yourself all keep it alive. Make room for it and the wave tends to complete quickly.

The technique came out of Alan Marlatt and Judith Gordon’s work on addiction in the 1980s. The same principle transfers to any urge at all — including the small ones that quietly cost athletes seasons.

How to do it
  1. Take note. Where do you feel the urge? Restless legs? Tight jaw? Heat in the chest? Hands wanting to move? Name it: “here’s an urge to…”
  2. Allow. Give it permission to be there. “Let it be.”
  3. Make room. Breathe around it. Let it rise and peak and fall in its own time.
  4. Expand. Keep the urge in view and widen out to the room, the body, the task.
  5. Score it as it moves. Out loud or silently: “it’s a 7… now an 8… still 8… now a 6.” Scoring is what turns it from a command into a wave you’re watching.
Worked example · a footballer, minute 63

Bad tackle, no card, and the urge to go after the player is instant and total. Take note: heat in the face, hands balled. Allow: “yep, there it is.” Score: a 9. He jogs back to position, still a 9. Ten seconds: 8. Twenty: 6. By the goal kick it’s a 4 and he’s in shape.

He didn’t calm down and he didn’t “let it go”. He just refused to hand the urge his legs for ninety seconds. That’s all it took.

Kind Hands & the Teammate Test

For: after a bad one. When the voice in your head is the harshest coach you’ve ever had.

What it is

Two moves that go together: kind words, and kind touch.

Let’s deal with the obvious objection first, because most athletes have it. The first time this gets suggested, a lot of people react the way one of Russ Harris’s clients did: “what’s this new-age rubbish?” Fair enough. Try it anyway and judge it on what happens, not on how it sounds.

Kind hands: place a hand on your chest or your stomach (or let it hover just above), and deliberately send warmth inward. That hand has held someone who was upset, or hugged a mate who was struggling. Put that same quality into it, aimed at yourself.

Kind words: say to yourself, silently, what you’d say to a teammate in exactly your position. Not a pep talk. Not a lie. Just the honest, warm thing you’d actually say to someone you rated.

Why it works

Every painful feeling is telling you something: that you care. There’s a gap between the reality you wanted and the reality you got, and the size of the pain tracks the size of that gap. Which means the hurt after a bad game isn’t a defect. It’s evidence that this matters to you.

Self-criticism and self-reassurance activate different systems — criticism engages error-processing and threat circuitry, warmth engages the systems tied to compassion and soothing. That’s why the harsh voice reliably keeps you agitated even when its content is accurate. And in athletes, self-compassion is consistently associated with better recovery from setbacks, more persistence, and less of the rumination that turns one bad performance into a bad month.

This is not letting yourself off. Self-compassion is not the same as low standards — if anything the evidence runs the other way, because people who aren’t terrified of their own reaction to failure look at their failures more honestly. You can review a shocking performance with complete rigour and not tear strips off yourself while you do it. Those are separate dials.

The Breath-Hold Experiment

For: proving to yourself — with your own data — that acceptance outperforms grit.

What it is

Everything else in this toolkit asks you to take our word for something counter-intuitive: that allowing discomfort gets you further than fighting it. This one doesn’t ask you to take anyone’s word for anything. It hands you a stopwatch and lets you find out.

You hold your breath twice. Round one: however you normally would — grit your teeth and battle the urge. Round two: same task, but you meet the discomfort with the skills from this toolkit instead of fighting it.

Then you compare your own two numbers.

Safety first, and this is not optional. Skip this entirely if you are pregnant, have any respiratory or cardiac condition, epilepsy, high blood pressure, a history of fainting, or any other reason breath-holding might not be safe for you. Never do it in or near water, while driving, or standing up. Sit down. Stop the moment you want to — the exercise works perfectly well at a mild level of discomfort, and nothing about it requires you to push to an extreme. If you’re unsure, don’t do it: the urge surfing tool teaches the same lesson with no physical demand at all.

Why it works

The urge to breathe is the cleanest laboratory for willingness you will ever get. It’s intense, it’s completely safe within sensible limits, it builds predictably, and — crucially — it is entirely internal. There is no opponent, no crowd, no coach, no scoreboard. Just you and a rising signal your body is generating.

Most people go longer on round two, and often substantially. Nothing about their lungs changed in the four minutes between attempts. What changed was how much of their attention went into the struggle rather than the task.

And there’s the whole model, demonstrated on your own body in about eight minutes. Not a metaphor. A measurement.

If your second number is lower

That happens, and it is not a failure. The usual reasons: you went hard on round one and hadn’t fully recovered; you turned round two into a competition with yourself, which is just the struggle switch wearing a disguise; or you were monitoring the clock instead of your body.

Rest properly, drop any goal of beating the first number, and try again another day. The number is the by-product. The skill is what you did while the urge was rising.

The Four Doors

For: seeing exactly what each of your four options does to your heading.

What it is

Picture yourself driving towards what matters to you. Then a passenger starts up — the doubt, the comparison, the voice that says don’t bother.

You have exactly four doors available. Everyone has a favourite, most people have never noticed which one it is, and two of the four quietly turn the wheel.

  1. Avoid. Ease away from it. Skip the session, pull out of the trial, give it eighty percent so a bad result doesn’t count. Rarely dramatic — usually just a small turn away.
  2. Argue. Turn round and fight it. Debate it, disprove it, shout it down. Feels strong. But notice where you have to look to do it: backwards, at the passenger, not forwards at the road.
  3. Allow. Hear it. Feel what it does to you, without flinching or wincing or fixing. Acknowledge that it’s aboard. Nothing more.
  4. Advance. Allow it — and take one deliberate step towards what matters anyway, while it’s still shouting.
Why it works

The insight isn’t that avoiding and arguing are “bad”. It’s about where your attention has to go to perform each one.

To avoid, you look away from your direction. To argue, you turn round and face the passenger. Both take your eyes off the road, and both feel productive at the time — arguing especially, because effort feels like progress.

Allow and advance are the only two that let you keep your eyes on where you’re going. That is the entire finding, and the widget below makes it visible rather than asking you to believe it.

On heading

Your passenger is shouting and you’re still pointed at what matters. Try each of the four doors and watch what it does to your heading.

The Rehearsal Room

For: the situation you dread. Meeting it fifty times in your head before you meet it once for real.

What it is

Take the moment you dread — the penalty, the start line, the weigh-in, the conversation with the coach — and rehearse it in your imagination in slow, deliberate sections. Not to make it go smoothly. To get thoroughly used to what it does to you.

Two things make this different from ordinary visualisation, and both matter.

First: you start well before the moment itself. Not at the penalty spot — at packing your bag the night before. The dread rarely begins where you think it does, and the early scenes are usually where it is actually manufactured.

Second: you rewind. When a scene stirs something up, you don’t push forward to get it over with. You go back a step or two and run it again, and again, until the charge comes down a little. Then you move on. Reaching the end is not the goal. Most people never get near the end, and it works anyway.

Why it works

This is exposure, done in imagination and paced by you. Repeatedly making contact with something difficult, while staying open to it rather than escaping, is the most reliably effective procedure in the whole of clinical psychology — and it doesn’t require the real situation to be available.

There is a striking piece of evidence for this in sport. University runners were split into two groups; one did a structured stress-rehearsal programme, the other simple relaxation. Their physical training was identical. The rehearsal group improved more over the season — and their aerobic capacity improved more too. Same running, different heads.

The mechanism is simple enough. A dreaded situation costs you attention twice: once in advance, and once during. Rehearsal drains the advance charge, so more of you is available on the day.

Work near your edge, not over it. Pick something that genuinely bothers you — around 5 or 6 out of 10 to start — not the worst thing that ever happened to you. If a scene takes you somewhere overwhelming, stop, drop anchor, and bring it to a session instead. This tool is for performance dread, not for trauma. If what you’re carrying is trauma, that deserves proper support and we will help you find it.

Worked example · a golfer, club championship

He doesn’t start at the first tee. He starts in his kitchen the night before, making dinner and thinking about getting an early night. Sink, lino, the pan. And a sick jolt in the stomach that shoots up towards his throat. He rates it an 8.

So he stays there. Notices where the feeling sits, whether it’s hot or cold, sharp or dull, moving or still. One slow breath. Then he rewinds to sitting in the lounge beforehand and runs the kitchen scene again. And again.

When it settles to a 6, he moves on — to lying in bed thinking about freezing on the first tee. Then back a step. Then forward.

Across a whole session he got as far as gripping the steering wheel on the drive to the club. He never reached the golf. That was a complete and successful rep.

10

DOTS

For: spotting, in the moment, that you’ve started moving away — before you’ve gone a long way.

What it is

Human beings have four broad ways of handling something we don’t want to feel. Four. That’s it — everything else is a variation.

DOTS is how you remember them:

  • D — Distract
  • O — Opt out
  • T — Time travel
  • S — Self-sabotage

Once you know the four, you can catch yourself doing one. And catching it is the entire point — because the move you can’t see is the one that runs your week.

Read this before the rest

None of these are bad

This is the most important sentence on the page, and the one people get wrong. DOTS is not a list of things you should stop doing. It is not a naughty list. Every single one of these strategies is sometimes exactly the right call.

Distraction is correct when you’re at half time and cannot afford to process the first half now. Opting out is wisdom when you’ve got a stress fracture and everyone’s telling you to play. Time travel is planning and learning — you cannot review a game or prepare for one without it. Even the fourth one exists because it works: it wouldn’t have a grip on anybody if it delivered nothing.

So the question is never “is this a DOTS?” The question is always “is this working, here, for what I actually care about?” Same behaviour, different day, different answer. That is what makes this an awareness tool and not a rulebook.

DDistract

Putting your attention somewhere else so you don’t have to be with what’s here. Phone, scrolling, gaming, box sets, music, banter, keeping busy, cleaning the whole house, extra sessions, anything that fills the gap.

In sport: rushing your next shot so you never have to feel the last one. Podcast on the whole drive home so the game doesn’t get a look in.

When it works: at half time. Mid-race. The night before, when there is genuinely nothing useful left to think about.

When it costs: when it’s three hours a night and the thing you’re avoiding is still there in the morning, slightly bigger.

OOpt out

Stepping back from the situation, the person or the challenge. Skipping it, pulling out, going quiet, not asking, not putting your hand up — or turning up and quietly giving less than everything so that a bad result doesn’t really count.

In sport: not asking for the ball when it’s tight. Not going to the physio because you don’t want to hear the answer. Eighty percent, so there’s always an excuse available.

When it works: pulling out of a fixture you’re genuinely injured for. Leaving an environment that’s harming you. Saying no to a fourth commitment.

When it costs: when your world quietly shrinks, one sensible-sounding decision at a time.

TTime travel

Leaving now and going somewhere in your head. Backwards into replaying, rehashing and regret — or forwards into worrying, rehearsing disasters, and “once I make the squad, then I’ll be alright.”

In sport: replaying the error for the next twenty minutes of a game you’re still playing. Taking the penalty four hundred times in bed the night before.

When it works: reviewing footage on Monday. Planning your week. Learning from a mistake once, deliberately, with a purpose.

When it costs: when you’re not learning any more, just re-feeling it. Or when the future you’re rehearsing hasn’t happened and mostly won’t.

SSelf-sabotage

The things you do to yourself to change how you feel, that carry a real cost. Drinking, vaping, eating to numb or not eating at all, over-training, skipping recovery, using self-criticism as a motivator, starting arguments, or deciding you don’t care before anyone can tell you you’re not good enough.

In sport: a big night out after a loss. Training through the injury so you never have to face rehab. Getting your excuse in early by not preparing properly.

When it works: honestly — in the short term, it does. It changes how you feel, fast. That is precisely why it sticks.

When it costs: almost always over time, and usually in the exact area you care most about.

Why it works

Two reasons.

One: it gives you a name for something that was invisible. Away moves rarely announce themselves. Nobody thinks “I am now avoiding.” They think “I’ll just check my phone”, “I’m too tired tonight”, “I’m just being realistic”. Four categories give you something to catch it with. Athletes routinely say the useful moment isn’t the log — it’s the first time they hear themselves think “that’s an O.”

Two: it forces the workability question. Most self-improvement hands you a list of bad habits and tells you to stop. That fails, because these behaviours are doing a job — and if you just remove them without acknowledging the job, you’ll find a replacement inside a week. DOTS asks something more useful: what did that give me, what did it cost me, and is that a trade I want to keep making?

This is the same workability test you met in Helpful vs True and in the choice point — and it’s the reason no behaviour can be listed permanently on either arrow.

Watch for this trap. The moment athletes learn DOTS, a lot of them start using it as a stick: “there I go again, distracting, I’m useless.” That’s just self-criticism wearing a new acronym — and it is itself an S. Noticing is meant to be curious, not damning. If you catch yourself getting judgemental with this tool, go and do Kind Hands and come back.

Worked example · the same behaviour, twice

Tuesday, 9pm. Two hours of scrolling after a rough session. Short term: numbed out, didn’t have to think about being dropped. Cost: bed at 1am, wrecked at training Wednesday, and the dropped thing still there untouched. Verdict: away.

Saturday, 5pm. Twenty minutes of scrolling in the changing room before kick-off, deliberately, because there is nothing useful left to think about and he knows he over-thinks in that gap. Short term: settled. Cost: none. Verdict: toward.

Identical behaviour. Same person. Same week. The behaviour was never the issue — the fit was. That’s the whole tool in one example.

Take it into the week

You don’t have to change a thing. For the first week, just name them — out loud or in your head. “That’s a D.” “There’s an O.” Four letters, no verdict, no telling yourself off.

The whole pillar in ten lines

  • The fight is the problem. Not the nerves, the doubt or the anger — the war you wage against them.
  • There is no delete button. Anyone selling you one is selling you a struggle strategy in a nicer box.
  • Name it to tame it. Putting an experience into words takes the heat out of it, every time.
  • You don’t have to change a thought to change its grip. Change the context instead.
  • Ask “is this useful?”, not “is this true?” You’ll never win the second argument.
  • The hurt means you care. Treat it accordingly.
  • Allowing beats gritting — and the breath-hold gives you your own data to prove it.
  • Avoiding and arguing both turn the wheel. Only allowing and advancing keep your eyes on the road.
  • Rehearse the lead-up, not just the moment. The dread is manufactured earlier than you think.
  • DOTS aren’t bad behaviours. They’re your four exits — and the only honest question is whether this one, this time, took you towards the athlete you want to be.
