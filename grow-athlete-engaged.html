Before you start

Two kids in the back
of a car

Both are being driven three hours to the same theme park. Both want exactly the same thing: to get there.

The first kid is fixed entirely on the goal. He’s on the edge of his seat, agitated, asking every ten minutes how much longer. The second kid has the same goal — but he’s also in touch with what he values: curiosity, playing, messing about. So he’s looking out of the window, playing games, waving at trucks.

They both arrive. They’re both happy. Only one of them enjoyed three hours of his life.

Now change the story: the car breaks down and neither gets there. Which one had the better day?

A heavily goal-focused life is a life of chronic discontentment — long stretches of dissatisfaction punctuated by brief hits of relief when you tick something off, which evaporate the moment the next target appears. That is a description of a great many athletic careers.

Values are not goals

A goal is something you can get, achieve or complete. Make the squad. Break 2:10. Win the title. You either get it or you don’t, and then it’s over.

A value is a quality you bring to how you behave. Being courageous. Being a good teammate. Being honest about where you’re at. You can never “complete” being courageous — but you can act on it in the next ten minutes, and every day for the rest of your life.

Which is why values hold when goals don’t. Injured? You can still be a good teammate. Deselected? You can still train with discipline. Retired? All of it still applies. Goals run out. Values don’t.

Why this pillar exists. In the ACT model, lack of values clarity plus inaction, impulsivity or avoidant persistence make up the inflexibility pillar called Disconnected. Values act as the selection criteria for behaviour, and committed action is how a change gets retained rather than lost. Values are described as the linchpin of the whole model — acceptance and defusion are never ends in themselves; they clear the path for a values-consistent life. Walser & Westrup, Learning ACT (2019), ch. 1, 6 & 7.

01

Finding Your Values

For: “I don’t know why I’m doing this any more.”

What it is

Values are your heart’s deepest desires for how you want to behave — how you want to treat yourself, other people, and the world around you. They’re verbs and adverbs, not nouns. Not “a good career” but “to work honestly”. Not “a great team” but “to support people generously”.

Five things they give you: wiser choices, because you’ve got a criterion. Direction, like an inner compass. Motivation that doesn’t need you to feel good first. Colour, when things are grey. And fulfilment — the sense of being true to yourself, available on any given Tuesday rather than only at a medal ceremony.

Values are not rules

This distinction matters enormously for athletes, because sport is drowning in rules dressed up as values.

A value is a direction: being committed. A rule is a command with a threat attached: I must never miss a session, no matter what. Rules contain must, should, have to, always, never, can’t unless.

Here’s how you tell them apart from the inside. When you act on a value, you feel meaning, purpose, vitality. When you’re obeying a rule, you feel pressure, obligation, heaviness, guilt. Same behaviour, sometimes. Completely different engine — and completely different long-run cost.

The more rigidly you obey a rule, the fewer options you have, and the more anxiety shows up if you ever bend it. That is a decent one-line description of how a committed athlete turns into a burnt-out one.

“But I haven’t been living like that, so it can’t be my value.” Very common, and wrong. Values describe how you want to behave. If you’d like to be a generous teammate — even if you’ve been a resentful one for six months — then generosity is already your value. It’s a quality of behaviour you desire, and you can start acting on it today. Your self-defeating behaviour isn’t evidence of your values; it’s evidence you got hooked and pulled away from them.

Take it into the week — flavouring and savouring

02

The Choice Point

For: seeing the whole pattern — what hooks you, where it takes you, and where you’d rather go.

What it is

A single map of how you operate under pressure. It has three parts.

At the bottom: the hooks. The difficult situations, thoughts, feelings, sensations, memories and urges that show up. You don’t choose these and you can’t stop them arriving.

The left arrow: away moves. What you do that takes you away from the athlete and person you want to be. Includes things you do in your head — worrying, ruminating, replaying.

The right arrow: toward moves. What you do that takes you towards them.

Two ways of getting hooked pull you left. OBEY: your thoughts and feelings command you — the thought “it’s hopeless” turns up and you stop trying. STRUGGLE: you fight them — anything to avoid, escape or suppress. Usually both at once.

The bit that catches everyone out

Any action can be a toward move or an away move, depending on the situation.

Extra gym session? Toward move if it’s part of a considered plan. Away move if you’re doing it at 10pm on a rest day to stop yourself feeling anxious about selection. Same behaviour. Opposite direction.

Watching video of your last game? Toward move if you’re looking for information. Away move if you’re on the ninth replay of the same error at 1am.

This is workability, and it’s the reason nobody else can fill in your choice point for you. Only you know which one you’re doing.

Feelings never go on the arrows. Emotions, sensations and urges always live at the bottom — they show up whether you like it or not. The arrows are for things you do. So “feeling anxious” goes at the bottom; “pulling out of the trial” goes on the away arrow. This distinction is the whole point of the map: you get a say over the arrows, not over the bottom.

03

The Four Arenas

For: when everything needs fixing at once and you don’t know where to start.

What it is

Nobody sculpts a statue by chipping at ten places simultaneously. One chip at a time, and eventually a block of stone becomes something.

Same with a life. Try to fix everything and you’ll be overwhelmed inside a week. So we split life into four arenas and work on one at a time.

  • Sport & work. Your sport, plus study, a job, and any training or learning you do.
  • People. Family, partner, teammates, friends, coaches — anyone you have or want a real bond with.
  • Play. Rest, recreation, fun, hobbies, the things you do that aren’t about performance. Athletes are notoriously terrible at this arena.
  • Self-care. Physical health, mental health, and if it’s relevant to you, spiritual or cultural life.

The four overlap, which is exactly what you want — it creates a domino effect. Small changes in one arena reliably improve the others.

04

Building a Real Goal

For: turning “I should sort myself out” into something that will actually happen.

The five rules
  1. Not too easy, not too hard. Too easy isn’t growth. Too hard and you’ll try, fail, and conclude you’re the problem. Find the middle.
  2. No emotional goals. “Feel confident before games” is not a goal — it’s a wish about your inner weather, and chasing it drags you straight back into struggle. Set a goal about what you’ll do.
  3. No dead person’s goals. If a corpse can do it better than you, it’s not a goal. “Stop getting angry with the ref” — a dead body has that nailed. Convert it: if I don’t do that, what will I do instead?
  4. Make it specific. Not “more recovery work” but “fifteen minutes of mobility, Tuesday and Thursday, straight after training, in the gym.” What, when, where.
  5. Make it realistic for your actual resources. Time, money, energy, health, skills, support. Not the resources of the version of you that gets eight hours’ sleep and has no exams.
Then two more questions that most goal-setting skips

What difficult thoughts and feelings will show up? Leaving your comfort zone produces discomfort — that’s not a sign of a bad plan, it’s the definition of the thing. Name in advance what your reason-giving machine will say.

Are you willing to make room for that in order to do this? If the honest answer is no, the goal is too big. Make it smaller, simpler, easier — until the answer is yes.

Write it down. Research consistently shows you are far more likely to follow through on a written goal than a thought one — and more likely again if you tell someone supportive and trustworthy what you’re going to do. That’s two minutes of effort for a large effect. Do both.

05

The HARD Barriers

For: after you said you’d do it and then didn’t.

What it is

You set a goal. You meant it. You didn’t do it. Now you feel guilty, and the guilt makes you less likely to try again.

First, the honest bit: this happens to everybody. Practitioners see it constantly. Authors of books about it do it. It is the human condition, not a character flaw. So unhook from “not good enough”, hold yourself kindly, and then do something more useful — work out which of the four barriers stopped you.

The four, and their antidotes
  1. H — Hooked. Your reason-giving machine talked you out of it. “I’m too tired.” “I’ll do it tomorrow.” “It won’t work anyway.”
    Antidote: unhooking skills. Notice and name it, thank your mind, or drop anchor. See the Open toolkit.
  2. A — Avoiding discomfort. Doing the thing means feeling something you’d rather not.
    Antidote: TAME it. And get into the habit of asking, “am I willing to make room for this in order to do what matters?”
  3. R — Remoteness from values. You’ve lost sight of why this matters, so there’s no fuel.
    Antidote: reconnect. Your values haven’t gone anywhere — a value is like your body: neglect it for years and it’s still there.
  4. D — Doubtful goals. The goal was too big, too fast, too perfect, or needed resources you don’t have.
    Antidote: break it down. Ask: what’s the smallest, easiest step that gets me even slightly closer? Then do that one.
The one question that cuts through

A client had every valid reason not to go for a morning run: he liked sleeping in, it was cold, he was unfit, his knees, he’d lost his shoes, he looked ridiculous in the gear.

So he was asked: “Suppose the person you love most in the world had been kidnapped, and the only way to get them back was to go for that run. Would you go?”

“Yes, obviously.”

“Even with all those perfectly valid reasons not to?”

That’s the point. You do not have to wait for the reasons to stop, or for your mind to become a cheerleader. You can have thirty perfectly good reasons not to do something, notice all of them, and go and do it anyway. (Adapted from Harris, ch. 24.)

Two proofs you don’t have to obey your thoughts

Say silently to yourself, “I can’t lift my arm, I can’t lift my arm” — and lift your arm.

Now say, “I have to close this page, I have to close this page” — and keep reading.

You just did the whole skill twice. The better you get at unhooking, the more often that gap between thought and action is available to you.

Activity · 8 minutes

Diagnose your barrier

Think of something you genuinely intended to do in the last month and didn’t. Work out which barrier — often it’s more than one — and apply the antidote.

1. What did you intend to do, and not do?

2. Which barrier stopped you?

Tap all that apply — sometimes it’s all four.

3. What exactly did your mind say in the moment?

The precise words. That’s your reason-giving machine’s greatest hits, and you’ll hear them again.

Saved

4. Apply the antidote. What are you changing?

Hooked → how you’ll unhook. Avoiding → what you’ll make room for. Remote → the value you’re reconnecting to. Doubtful → the smaller version.

Saved

5. The smallest, easiest step.

So small it feels almost insulting. That is the correct size. How do you eat an elephant? One mouthful at a time.

6. And what did you say to yourself about not doing it?

If it was harsh, rewrite it here the way you’d say it to a teammate.

Source. The HARD barriers, the kidnap question and the arm-lifting demonstrations: Harris, The Happiness Trap (2022), ch. 24.

06

The Brave Action Ladder

For: the thing you keep not doing because you’re waiting to feel ready.

What it is

Brave is not a feeling. It’s a doing word.

The mistake almost every athlete makes at some point is treating confidence as a prerequisite — once I feel ready, I’ll ask for the ball / tell the coach / try the harder line. But confidence is a by-product of doing the thing, not an entry requirement for it. Wait for the feeling and you’ll wait a long time, because the feeling is downstream of the action.

So we build a ladder. Small brave actions first, bigger ones later, each one done with the discomfort rather than after it’s gone.

Why it works

This is exposure with a values-based purpose. You are deliberately making contact with something uncomfortable, and staying with it, while doing something that matters to you. Two things change: your nervous system gets direct evidence that the discomfort is survivable, and you accumulate real proof that you can act independently of how you feel. That second one is worth more than any amount of positive self-talk.

It also explains why real toughness is not what most sporting cultures taught. Toughness built on fear and hiding weakness is fragile — performing out of fear reliably makes performance worse. Toughness built on doing the hard thing while feeling the hard feeling is the version that holds.

The rungs must be genuinely small. A ladder that starts at rung seven isn’t a ladder, it’s a wall. If your first step doesn’t feel slightly embarrassingly easy, it’s too big.

3. Build the rungs — easiest first.

Add five, from “almost too easy” to “the actual thing”.

4. What will show up when you take rung one?

The whole pillar in six lines

  • Goals run out. Values don’t. One is a destination, the other is a direction you keep walking.
  • Values are not rules. Meaning and vitality versus pressure and guilt. Learn to tell them apart from the inside.
  • Any action can go either way. Only you can judge whether what you’re doing is working.
  • One arena at a time. Try to fix everything and you’ll fix nothing.
  • If it’s under 7 out of 10 realistic, make it smaller. Every time.
  • Brave is a doing word. Confidence follows the action; it doesn’t authorise it.
