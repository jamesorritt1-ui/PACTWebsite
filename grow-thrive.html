THRIVE

A Psychological Flexibility Program for Young Athletes

8 modules  ·  ~8 weeks  ·  evidence-based ACT  ·  mobile-first

From "toughen up" to thriving. THRIVE teaches three moves — Acknowledge, Connect, Engage — that help you do what matters even when your head and body throw everything at you. In sport first. Then everywhere else.

Explore the program See how it works

One skill set. Every arena of your life.

Why THRIVE

The old playbook
backfires

For decades, young athletes were told to "harden up," suppress emotion, project confidence, and bulldoze through pain. The science no longer supports this. As performance scientist Steve Magness puts it, real toughness is "experiencing discomfort or distress, leaning in, paying attention, and creating space to take thoughtful action" — the opposite of false bravado, which is built on fear and hiding any sign of weakness.

Athletes who perform out of fear perform worse. The best performers have a flexible, adaptive coping ability — they bounce between strategies depending on what the moment demands. That's emotional flexibility, not emotional suppression.

THRIVE turns this shift into a structured digital program built on Acceptance & Commitment Training (ACT) and its central target: psychological flexibility — the ability to be present, open up, and do what matters, even alongside difficult thoughts and feelings. That capacity predicts not just performance, but mental health, study success, and the quality of your relationships across life. That dual relevance — performance and life — is the heart of THRIVE.

The structural heart of the program

ACE → the TriFlex

Most ACT products bolt together a loose collection of exercises. THRIVE is different: every module hangs off a single, memorable spine you can carry in your head for life. That spine is ACE, and it maps one-to-one onto the three pillars of psychological flexibility — Russ Harris's TriFlex.

The genius of the spine: ACE is at once a 30-second grounding skill you can run on the start line, the table of contents for the whole program, and a complete, portable model of psychological flexibility. The same three moves you use before a penalty also work before an exam, a hard conversation, a job interview, or a 2am spiral of worry. One skill set, every arena. Learning design & UX philosophy

Built for how
you actually learn

THRIVE is designed for 16–24 year-olds — older teens and emerging adults — so the tone is adult, autonomy-supportive and identity-aware. Every design choice is evidence-led, and engineered against the number-one enemy of any digital program: drop-off.

Short by design

Every core teaching video is capped at ~6 minutes — the point where engagement falls off a cliff. Content arrives in learner-paced chunks, each wrapped in active learning: a question before, an interactive task after.

Motivation that lasts

Grounded in Self-Determination Theory: autonomy (you choose your path, dose and focus), competence (scaffolded skills, visible progress) and relatedness (a warm, human voice working with you). Reminders are values-framed, never nagging.

Accessible & mobile-first

Captions and transcripts for every video, an audio-only, eyes-free version of every practice, dyslexia-friendly type, light/dark modes, and a phone-first layout — because this audience lives on their phones.

The program

Eight modules
+ onboarding

Designed to be completed roughly one module per week over eight weeks — though the autonomy-supportive design lets you move faster or slower. The arc is deliberate: understand the problem, learn each ACE move in turn, then put them together for life. Tap any module to see the full detail.

By the end you can: explain what psychological flexibility is in your own words, locate the ACE → TriFlex map, set up your practice environment, and (optionally) complete a baseline measure.

Core concept

A warm 3-minute welcome from the clinical psychologist behind The Performance Act, framed as a collaboration, not a lecture: "This isn't about toughening up or thinking positive. It's about learning three moves — Acknowledge, Connect, Engage — that help you do what matters even when your head and body are throwing everything at you. We'll use them in sport first, then everywhere else." You then meet the interactive ACE → TriFlex map — the home screen you return to all program long.

Digital interactions
  • A "What brings you here?" selector (performance pressure, anxiety, confidence, focus, motivation, identity, wellbeing) that lightly personalises examples downstream.
  • A practice-setup checklist — headphones, a quiet 10 minutes, notifications snoozed.
  • Choice of avatar / nickname for any social features.
Multimedia

Welcome video (<3 min, captioned); animated map; a 60-second "how to get the most from this" micro-guide.

Apply in Sport

Orientation to using ACE on the start line, between reps, and in the changing room.

Apply in Life

The selector explicitly includes study/work stress, relationships, sleep and mood — signalling from minute one that this is a life program.

Lock it in: optional baseline self-assessment and a single commitment — "I'll give this 10 minutes, most days."

Open the full module Interactive walkthrough with reflection prompts and your baseline check-in.

By the end you can: explain why the mind generates "mental noise," describe experiential avoidance and why suppression backfires, and articulate the ACT alternative — workability over control.

Core concept

The human mind evolved to detect threats and solve problems, so it chatters, criticises and catastrophises — what athletes call "mental noise." The problem isn't the noise; it's the struggle with it. Fighting, suppressing or avoiding inner experience (experiential avoidance) tends to amplify it and pull you away from what matters.

Experiential exercises
  • The "Don't think about it" demonstration — the classic thought-suppression task: try hard not to think about a specific worry for 60 seconds, then notice what happened. Insight delivered experientially, not lectured.
  • The "Struggle Switch" simulation (adapted from Harris): a slider showing how turning up the struggle with anxiety turns up suffering — while the anxiety itself stays roughly the same.
Multimedia

A ~5-minute animated explainer — "Why your mind won't shut up — and why that's normal" — plus a montage of elite athletes describing pressure, opened with a guiding question: "Watch how they relate to nerves — do they fight them, or make space for them?"

Apply in Sport

Journaling prompt — "Name a recent moment when fighting your nerves made things worse."

Apply in Life

"Where else do you do this? Replaying an awkward text? Forcing yourself to sleep? Avoiding an assignment?" Log one non-sport example — the transfer habit starts here.

Lock it in: a one-screen summary card and a retrieval question — "In your words, what's the cost of the struggle switch being on?"

Open the full module Interactive walkthrough with the suppression experiment, reflection prompts and a Struggle Switch self-check.

By the end you can: define acceptance (making room) versus resignation, practise dropping the struggle with an uncomfortable sensation, and connect acceptance to the A of ACE and the Be Open pillar.

Core concept

Acknowledge begins with acceptance — willingly making room for difficult feelings instead of fighting them. This is explicitly not giving up; it's leaning in to discomfort so you can take thoughtful action. We anchor the language: "This is the A in ACE, and the 'Be Open' corner of your map."

Experiential exercises
  • A guided acceptance practice (audio, ~6 min): notice an uncomfortable sensation (pre-competition butterflies, restlessness), locate it, breathe around it, and drop the rope in the tug-of-war with it.
  • The "Tug-of-War with a Monster" metaphor as a short animation, then a reflective prompt.
  • A body-map interaction: tap where in the body you feel pressure and label the sensation — building interoceptive awareness and the habit of naming ("Here's tension," "Here's anxiety").
Multimedia

Animated metaphor (<3 min); audio-guided acceptance practice with an eyes-free option; a downloadable one-page "Be Open" reference.

Apply in Sport

"Before your next session, name the feeling you usually try to get rid of, and practise making room for it for 60 seconds."

Apply in Life

Generalised to homesickness, social anxiety, exam dread, grief after deselection or injury: "Choose one uncomfortable feeling outside sport this week and practise dropping the rope." The same willingness that lets you compete also lets you live.

Lock it in: a streak-tracked daily 1-minute "make room" practice; a retrieval card distinguishing acceptance from resignation.

Open the full module Interactive walkthrough with a moving tug-of-war visual, the Drop the Rope practice, a name-it activity and a 7-day Make Room journal.

By the end you can: explain cognitive fusion vs defusion, use at least two defusion techniques, and recognise that you don't have to believe or obey a thought to notice it.

Core concept

The second half of Be Open is defusion — stepping back from thoughts and seeing them as words and images passing through, rather than commands or truths. "I'm not good enough," seen clearly, is a thought, not a fact. This completes the A of ACE: Acknowledge means noticing both feelings (acceptance) and thoughts (defusion) with curiosity.

Experiential exercises
  • "Passengers on the Bus" — the signature ACT metaphor, adapted from the program's culturally-resonant "Passengers on our Waka/Bus." You're the driver heading toward your values; difficult thoughts are noisy passengers who can shout but cannot steer. An interactive scene lets you "drive toward what matters" while passengers (self-doubt, comparison, fear of failure) pipe up — and you practise acknowledging them without handing over the wheel.
  • Quick defusion drills: silently retitling a thought ("I'm noticing the thought that…"), the "thank your mind" technique, and saying a sticky thought in a silly voice or singing it to drain its power.
Multimedia

Interactive "bus" animation; a ~4-minute video naming common athlete thinking traps (perfectionism, catastrophising, personalising); an audio defusion practice.

Apply in Sport

"Name your loudest passenger. Next training session, notice it, thank your mind, and keep driving."

Apply in Life

Defusion applied to social comparison (especially on social media), imposter feelings at university or a first job, and self-criticism: "Catch one harsh self-statement this week and defuse it." Directly relevant to the identity exploration of your twenties.

Lock it in: a saved "name your passengers" list you revisit later; a retrieval quiz on fusion vs defusion.

Open the full module Interactive walkthrough with the moving Passengers on the Bus, a personal Defuser tool, reflection questions and a week of practice.

By the end you can: practise flexible present-moment attention, use grounding to steady yourself under pressure, experience self-as-context (the "observer self"), and link all of this to the C of ACE and the Be Present pillar.

Core concept

Connect is coming back into your body and the present moment — the antidote to autopilot, rumination about the past and worry about the future. It has two layers: simple grounding (present-moment contact) and the deeper, stabilising sense of the observer self (self-as-context) — the "you" that has been noticing thoughts and feelings all along and is bigger than any of them.

Experiential exercises
  • A guided grounding / anchoring practice built on Harris's dropping-anchor C-step: push feet into the floor, straighten the spine, notice 5 things you see and 3 you hear.
  • An anchor-building task: choose a personal visual anchor and physical anchor (the knurling of the barbell, boots gripping the turf, a spot on the horizon) to re-focus attention during competition.
  • A short mindful-movement practice, and the "Sky & Weather" metaphor for self-as-context: thoughts and feelings are the weather; you are the sky that holds it all and is never damaged by the storm.
Multimedia

Audio-guided dropping-anchor practice in 1-, 3- and 6-minute versions (autonomy through dose); a "Sky & Weather" animation; a mindful-movement video.

Apply in Sport

"Build your two anchors and use them between points / reps / lifts this week."

Apply in Life

Grounding for panic before a presentation, settling before sleep, staying present in a conversation instead of rehearsing your reply, breaking a doom-scroll: "Drop anchor once a day in an ordinary moment — brushing teeth, waiting for the kettle."

Lock it in: your two anchors are saved to your profile; a daily anchor streak; a retrieval prompt — "What three things does Connect do for you?"

Open the full module Three hands-on exercises — a follow-along breathing pacer, a 5-4-3-2-1 drop-anchor drill and your own anchors — plus a Sky & Weather visual and a week of journaling.

By the end you can: distinguish values from goals, identify 3–5 core values across life domains, and see how values give direction in and out of sport — the E of ACE.

Core concept

Engage starts with knowing what you're engaging toward. Values are your chosen directions — how you want to behave and what you want to stand for — not destinations to be ticked off. A goal can be achieved ("win the final"); a value is a direction you can always move in ("compete with courage and integrity").

Experiential exercises
  • The "80th Birthday / Retirement Speech" exercise: imagine the people who matter describing who you were and how you showed up — what would you want them to say?
  • A digital values card-sort: swipe a deck (courage, connection, growth, discipline, honesty, creativity, fairness, fun…) into "very important / quite / not for me," then narrow to a top five.
  • A values-across-domains grid: place your values into sport, study/work, relationships and self — making the life-wide scope concrete and visible.
Multimedia

A reflective ~5-minute video on values vs goals (with the warning that chasing goals while ignoring values is a fast route to burnout); an audio values visualisation; a digital values-collage option.

Apply in Sport

"Name the values you most want to bring to training — independent of results."

Apply in Life

The grid is the transfer: values are identified in four life domains at once. "This is you figuring out who you are and what you stand for — exactly the task of your twenties." Prompt: "Pick one value and one tiny way to live it outside sport this week."

Lock it in: your top-five values are saved to your profile and surfaced in later modules; a retrieval card on values vs goals.

Open the full module A Value-or-Goal game, an interactive values card-sort that saves your top five, a values-across-your-life grid, the 80th-birthday journal and a week of practice.

By the end you can: translate values into specific committed actions, use habit design to make valued action stick, and handle the discomfort that shows up when you act on your values.

Core concept

The second half of Do What Matters is committed action — taking values off the page and into behaviour, again and again, especially when it's uncomfortable. Values without action are just nice words; action without values is just busyness.

Experiential exercises
  • A values-to-action ladder: pick a value → name a long-term goal → a short-term goal → one thing you can do today.
  • A habit-design workshop built on the four laws (make it obvious, attractive, easy, satisfying) — e.g., designing a consistent sleep or training routine.
  • A "willingness" check: because committed action usually triggers the very thoughts and feelings from Modules 2–4, you rehearse pairing action with Acknowledge + Connect ("I can feel the fear and send the message / do the rep"). The first explicit integration of all three ACE moves.
Multimedia

Video on committed action and willingness (<6 min); an interactive habit-loop builder; an audio "rehearsal" combining willingness with a valued action.

Apply in Sport

"Choose one values-based training action and design the habit around it this week."

Apply in Life

Applied to studying, reaching out to a friend, a hard conversation, a health behaviour, asking for help: "Take one small values-based action outside sport you've been avoiding." Avoidance is normal — willingness, not motivation, is the engine.

Lock it in: a saved action plan; a streak for your chosen habit; retrieval — "What's the difference between motivation and willingness?"

By the end you can: perform the full dropping-anchor ACE sequence fluently, use a "choice point" to navigate pressure moments, and build a personal performance routine using ACE.

Core concept

Now the three moves become one fluid skill. You run the complete cycle — Acknowledge (open to thoughts and feelings), Connect (come back to body and present), Engage (turn to what matters and act) — and learn to deploy it in seconds under pressure, or slowly over several minutes when needed.

Experiential exercises
  • A scenario simulator (a matured "Choice Points" game): face branching sport and life situations — a poor warm-up, a hostile crowd, a bad night's sleep, a fight with a partner before a game — and choose ACE responses, seeing how each "choice point" moves you toward or away from your values.
  • A build-your-own performance routine template: sequence your pre-performance ACE steps (acknowledge nerves → anchor with chosen cues → engage with the first task).
  • A guided full ACE practice at three durations — 30-second, 3-minute and extended.
Multimedia

A flagship ~6-minute video walking through dropping anchor end-to-end with a real athlete demonstration; the interactive choice-point simulator; a downloadable ACE routine card to screenshot or print.

Apply in Sport

"Use your ACE routine before every session and competition this week, and log what happened."

Apply in Life

The same routine for exams, interviews, performances, conflict and overwhelm: "Use the full ACE cycle in one high-pressure non-sport moment." The core promise, delivered: one skill, every arena.

Lock it in: your performance routine is saved to your profile; the ACE skill-tree shows all three branches complete; a cumulative retrieval quiz.

By the end you can: plan to maintain practice, recognise and recover from "off-track" periods, and integrate psychological flexibility into your identity and life beyond sport.

Core concept

Skills fade without practice, and everyone drifts off-track. This module reframes lapses as normal and teaches recovery, not perfection. It deliberately addresses identity beyond sport — a critical wellbeing issue for this age group: athletic identity is real but narrow, and psychological flexibility protects against the identity crises of injury, deselection, transition and retirement.

Experiential exercises
  • An "on-track / off-track" reflection mapping what living the model looks like for you.
  • A self-as-context revisit: "You are not your sport, not your results, not your thoughts — you're the one who's been showing up to all of it."
  • A flexible self-talk & self-compassion practice — choosing how you speak to yourself the way you'd speak to a teammate.
  • A future-self letter: write to yourself for a hard day six months out, integrating your values and ACE plan.
Multimedia

A closing video on thriving (not just performing) and building a life bigger than results; an audio self-compassion practice; a personalised, auto-generated "Your ACE Toolkit" summary pulling together your saved values, anchors, passengers, routine and action plan.

Apply in Sport

"Write your one-line plan for staying on track across a season."

Apply in Life

Explicit treatment of transfer — study/work, relationships, mental health, identity and quarter-life uncertainty: "Name three non-sport situations next month where you'll use ACE." The closing message: even if you stop competing tomorrow, you keep these tools for life.

Lock it in: an optional re-assessment with a before/after view; a downloadable certificate/toolkit; and an open invitation to revisit any module anytime — THRIVE stays a reference, not a one-time course.

Optional, opt-in, private

Measure your
growth

Measurement is always opt-in and for your own insight — never a test or a gate. A brief, validated, process-focused measure is offered at baseline, midpoint and end, mapped directly onto the three pillars THRIVE teaches.

Be Open+
Be Present+
Do What Matters+
The validated measure

The CompACT (Comprehensive assessment of ACT processes; Francis et al., 2016) is the recommended primary measure because its subscales — Openness to Experience, Behavioural Awareness, Valued Action — mirror Be Open / Be Present / Do What Matters exactly. (We deliberately avoid the older AAQ-II, which conflates inflexibility with general distress.)

Lightweight weekly check-in

A 0–10 "How open / present / engaged did I feel this week?" — one item per pillar — plus a practice-streak log and an optional values-consistency rating. Frequent feedback that feeds your sense of progress.

Private by default

All scores stay private to you, with a plain-language note that THRIVE is educational, not a substitute for clinical care, alongside clear signposting to support services.

Evidence-informed, honestly

Built on the
research

THRIVE is positioned as an evidence-informed development program, not a treatment. The science behind both ACT-in-sport and digital delivery is real and growing — and we're upfront about its limits.

RCT
In a randomised controlled trial of 34 junior elite ice-hockey players (Lundgren et al., 2021), ACT training improved objective performance and blinded coach ratings — effects that lasted at 3-month follow-up. Every ACT-trained player recommended it to others.
39
A meta-analysis of 39 RCTs (Han & Kim, 2022) found internet-based ACT improves anxiety, stress, distress and quality of life — and that lightly guided versions outperform fully self-guided ones, which is why guidance is built in as an option.
~6 min
Across 6.9M video sessions (Guo et al., 2014), engagement stayed near 100% for videos under six minutes and collapsed past twelve — so every core THRIVE video is capped at ~6 minutes and wrapped in active learning.
