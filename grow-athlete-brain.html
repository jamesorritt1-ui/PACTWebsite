Before you start

You cannot think
your way out of exhaustion

Nobody in sport psychology likes admitting this, but it’s true: the single most common reason an athlete’s mental skills stop working is not that the skills are wrong. It’s that they are being run on four hours’ sleep, no breakfast and a fortnight of not talking to anyone.

There’s good evidence for this from an unexpected direction. Researchers asked people to estimate how high a bar they could step over. Then they asked a group who had been kept awake for twenty-four hours. The sleep-deprived group badly underestimated what they could physically do — their actual capability hadn’t changed, only their reading of it. As one sport psychologist put it: we perceive the environment in terms of the costs of acting within it.

In other words, when you’re depleted, everything looks harder than it is. The hill looks steeper. The opponent looks better. The conversation looks more dangerous. And you will interpret all of that as evidence about you, when it is actually evidence about your recovery.

Why this sits inside a psychology resource

Psychological flexibility is described in the ACT literature as a multidimensional business — you cannot sensibly build a healthy emotional life while ignoring diet, sleep and exercise, because those are what make a vital life physically possible. It is one system. This page is the other half of the same job.

Pick one. Six behaviours is far too many to change at once, and trying will guarantee you change none of them. Work through the audits, find your worst one, and give it a fortnight. The others will benefit anyway — these things pull each other along.

01

Sleep

The single biggest performance lever most athletes are leaving on the table.

Why it matters

While you sleep, your brain consolidates the motor skills you drilled that day, clears metabolic waste, regulates emotion, and restores attention. Nothing you can buy comes close. When athletes deliberately extend their sleep towards ten hours in bed, sprint times, shooting accuracy, reaction time and mood all measurably improve.

The emotional side matters just as much here. Short sleep reliably increases reactivity and lowers your tolerance for frustration — which means a bad night doesn’t just cost you a step of pace, it costs you the composure you were relying on your mental skills to provide.

8–10 hrs
Teens, 13–18

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommendation for optimal health.

7–9 hrs
Young adults, 18–25

More than nine is appropriate when training hard or paying back sleep debt.

9–10+ hrs
Hard-training athletes

Sleep-in-sport researchers suggest aiming higher. Some elite performers bank 10–12.

And the reality gap: studies find adolescent athletes average around seven hours a night, and student-athletes often sleep roughly two hours less than non-athletes. That is a large, fixable edge sitting on the table.

More than just hours — RU-SATED

Sleep researchers describe sleep health across six dimensions. It’s a useful way to find which bit of yours is actually broken.

  • Regularity — similar bed and wake times day to day. Start here. This is the highest-value habit there is.
  • Satisfaction — sleep feels restful.
  • Alertness — you can stay awake and focused during the day.
  • Timing — you’re asleep at hours that suit your life.
  • Efficiency — you fall asleep and stay asleep reasonably easily.
  • Duration — enough hours for your age and training load.
And the ACT bit: stop fighting to fall asleep

Everything in the Open toolkit applies at 2am. Lying there trying to force sleep is the struggle switch at full power — and it manufactures exactly the arousal that keeps you awake. You cannot will yourself unconscious.

What you can do is make room for being awake. Drop anchor. Let the frustration be there. Notice the “I’m going to be wrecked tomorrow” story and thank your mind for it. Kind hands, if you have them. The aim is to stop the fight, not to win it.

02

Fuel

Your brain runs on glucose. Under-fuel and the first thing you lose is judgement.

Why it matters

Your brain is roughly 2% of your body weight and consumes something like 20% of your energy. It has almost no storage. When available fuel drops, the functions that go first are the expensive ones: sustained attention, decision-making under pressure, and emotional regulation.

Which is why under-fuelled athletes are not just slower — they are shorter-tempered, more easily hooked by unhelpful thoughts, and worse at reading a game. If someone tells you they’ve “lost their bottle” and they last ate at 7am, take the food history before you take the psychology.

The four things that actually matter
  1. Eat enough, full stop. Chronic under-fuelling — whether deliberate or just disorganised — is the most common and most damaging nutrition problem in sport. It costs bone health, hormonal health, immune function, mood and eventually your season.
  2. Fuel around the work. Something with carbohydrate 1–3 hours before training. Something with carbohydrate and protein reasonably soon after. This is the highest-yield timing change most athletes can make.
  3. Don’t skip breakfast on training days. It is the most commonly skipped meal and the one most likely to be followed by a session.
  4. Build the plate. Carbohydrate for fuel, protein for repair, fruit and vegetables for everything else, some fat. It genuinely is that boring.

An honest boundary. We are a sport psychology practice, not dietitians. This page covers the behavioural basics only. Anything to do with body composition targets, restrictive eating, weight-making, or a relationship with food that has started to feel distressing or controlling belongs with a registered sports dietitian and, where relevant, a clinician — and we will help you get there. Please bring it to a session rather than working it out alone.

03

Hydration

The cheapest, most-ignored variable in the whole list.

Why it matters

Fluid loss of around 2% of body mass — about 1.5 kg for a 75 kg athlete, which is an ordinary hard session in warm conditions — is enough to measurably impair endurance performance, and there is reasonable evidence it also degrades concentration, mood and skill execution before you feel especially thirsty.

The psychology angle: mild dehydration produces low mood, irritability and a heavy-legged, can’t-be-bothered feeling that is very easy to misread as a motivation problem. Athletes routinely conclude they’ve gone soft when they’ve simply gone dry.

How to actually judge it
  • Urine colour, first thing. Crude, free, and good enough. Pale straw is the target.
  • Body mass before and after a session. Every 1 kg lost is roughly 1 litre of fluid. This is the only way to know your sweat rate rather than a number off a poster.
  • Thirst. Useful, but it lags — by the time you’re properly thirsty you’re already behind.

Replace roughly 1.25–1.5 litres for every kilogram lost across the hours after a session, and include some sodium if you’re a salty or heavy sweater or it’s hot.

More is not better. Drinking far beyond your losses is not harmless — overdrinking during prolonged exercise can cause hyponatraemia, which is dangerous. Aim to roughly match your losses, not to drown them. And note the obvious: some urine colour changes come from supplements (B vitamins especially) rather than hydration.

04

Social Connection

The behaviour athletes cut first, and the one they can least afford to lose.

Why it matters

This is not the soft one on the list. In a meta-analysis covering more than three million people, social isolation and loneliness were associated with substantially increased mortality risk — effects comparable in size to well-established risk factors. Connection is not a nice-to-have. It is a health behaviour, in the same category as sleep.

For athletes there’s a specific problem. Sport eats your time, and the first things to go are the relationships that have nothing to do with performance. Then something goes wrong — injury, deselection, a bad run — and you discover that you narrowed your whole identity and your whole social world onto one thing, at exactly the moment that thing became painful.

There’s also good evidence about what actually builds team belonging: not team-bonding days or big speeches, but the accumulation of small everyday moments that either include people or quietly exclude them.

The self-determination angle

Motivation research identifies three basic psychological needs: autonomy (having a say), competence (getting better at something), and relatedness (belonging with people). Sport is usually excellent at the middle one and can be dreadful at the other two. Relatedness is the one most often starved — and when it is, motivation quietly drains away, which people then misdiagnose as not wanting it enough.

Withdrawing is a struggle strategy. Going quiet when things are hard feels protective and it is the most common away move athletes make. It reliably makes everything worse, and it’s the one your teammates and family notice long before you admit it. If you recognise yourself here, that is worth naming in your next session.

05

Movement & Recovery

You already train. This is about the other half of the equation.

Why it matters

You are an athlete, so you are not short of movement. What most athletes are short of is recovery — and specifically, the recognition that training stress and life stress land in the same account.

Exams, a house move, a relationship ending, a part-time job, a long commute: all of that is load. Your body does not keep separate ledgers for “training stress” and “everything else stress”. When the total exceeds what you can absorb, you get the classic picture — flat, irritable, sleeping badly, performing worse the harder you push, and increasingly convinced the answer is to push harder still.

There is a second, less obvious point. Movement that is not training — a walk, a swim with no watch on, getting outside — does something training doesn’t. Research consistently shows that time in nature broadens perspective, shifts difficult situations from being read as threats to being read as challenges, and speeds up recovery from stress. It is one of the most reliable ways to zoom out.

Signals that your total load is too high
  • Resting heart rate creeping up, or sleep getting worse despite going to bed earlier
  • Performance dropping while effort increases
  • Losing enjoyment for a sport you used to love
  • Snapping at people, or nothing feeling worth doing
  • Picking up niggles and small illnesses more than usual
  • Needing more and more to feel anything at all

Any two of those, persisting for a couple of weeks, is a conversation to have — with your coach, with us, and if it’s physical, with a doctor.

06

Quiet & Screens

Learning to be alone in your own head without immediately reaching for something.

Why it matters

Here is an uncomfortable finding that Magness draws on: people are so unwilling to sit alone with their own thoughts that, in one famous set of studies, a substantial number chose to give themselves an electric shock rather than spend fifteen minutes doing nothing.

Most of us don’t have that option, so we reach for a phone instead — and the average person now does it hundreds of times a day. The result is that we have almost completely lost the skill of tolerating our own company.

That matters for an athlete more than for most people, because sport routinely puts you alone with discomfort and no exit. The last 400 metres. The tunnel. The physio bed for four months. Lying awake the night before. If your only strategy for an uncomfortable inner state is to reach for a distraction, then in every moment that matters most, you are unarmed.

Distraction isn’t the enemy

This is important and often misunderstood. Distraction is a legitimate strategy — a low-cost one that works well early, before something has picked up momentum. The best performers use it. What separates them is that they can also use the more demanding strategies — turning towards a sensation, reappraising it, staying with it — when the moment calls for that instead.

Novices default to distraction because when intensity gets high, it’s all they’ve got. Elite performers can still direct attention into an experience while it’s screaming at them to look away. That capacity is built through training — and quiet is the training ground.

This is not a lecture about screen time. The question isn’t how many hours you’re on your phone. It’s whether you have any reps at all of being alone with your own head — and whether the reaching is a choice or a reflex.
