01 · The move

Not a course to finish —
a way to keep

You’ve built the whole skill. This last module isn’t about learning something new; it’s about keeping it — for the long haul, and for the whole of your life, not just your sport.

Two honest truths up front. First, skills fade without practice — ACE is a set of muscles, and muscles need reps. Second, everyone drifts off-track. You will have days, weeks, maybe seasons where you forget all of this and get swept away. That’s not failure — it’s being human. The goal was never perfection; it’s recovery: noticing you’ve drifted, and gently coming back.

"You don’t need to never fall off. You need to get good at climbing back on — a little faster, a little kinder, each time."

— Living the model

And one more thing this module does: it shows you that ACE doesn’t replace the classic sport-psychology tools you may already use — visualisation, routines, self-talk, goal-setting. It upgrades them. Most of those tools were built on a hidden control agenda: get rid of the nerves, force the confidence, block the bad thoughts. Run them that way and they eventually backfire. Run them the ACT way — in service of being open, present and values-driven — and they become genuinely powerful.

02 · The big integration

Your sport-psych tools,
the ACT way

Here are the tools you’ve probably met before — each one shown two ways: the old control agenda that quietly backfires, and the ACT-consistent version that actually works. Tap any tool to open it. The test for every tool is the same: does it help you be more open, present and values-driven — or is it just another way to fight your own experience?

The old agenda

Only picture success — see yourself winning, feeling great, and erase any “negative” image. Believe it hard enough and it’ll come true.

The ACT-consistent way

Rehearse values-based action under difficulty, not a fantasy highlight reel. Picture the nerves, the bad start, the hostile crowd — and see yourself run ACE and execute your process anyway. That’s coping imagery, and it transfers to the real thing because the real thing has discomfort in it.

Try this

Before a big event, run a 2-minute mental rep: feel the pressure show up, drop anchor, and watch yourself do your first job well. Rehearse the choice point, not just the trophy.

The old agenda

A fixed ritual to manufacture a feeling — get “in the zone,” force confidence, make yourself calm. When the feeling doesn’t come, the routine feels broken.

The ACT-consistent way

A routine whose job is to reliably run ACE — not to produce a mood. It works whether you feel amazing or awful: Acknowledge what’s here, Connect (your anchor), Engage (your process cue). That’s exactly the routine you built in Module 08.

Try this

Use your saved ACE routine before every performance, and judge it on whether you ran the steps — never on whether it made you feel a certain way.

The old agenda

Replace negative thoughts with positive affirmations — “I’m confident, I’ll win” — and argue the bad ones away. Often it feels fake, and fighting a thought just feeds it.

The ACT-consistent way

It was never positive vs negative — it’s workable vs unworkable, held lightly. Defuse from unhelpful thoughts (Module 03) instead of wrestling them, and use short process cues tied to your values (“feet,” “next ball,” “compete”). Talk to yourself like a good coach: firm and kind.

Try this

Pick one process cue word. When the noise comes: thank your mind, say your cue, do the job. You don’t have to believe a nicer thought — you just don’t obey the unhelpful one.

The old agenda

Chase outcome goals — win, place, hit the number. Success equals the result, and your worth rides on it.

The ACT-consistent way

Goals serve values, and you hold them lightly — full commitment, no white-knuckle attachment. Weight process and controllable goals (effort, behaviour) above outcomes. Exactly what you built across Modules 05–06: value → direction → the next rep.

Try this

For your next event, set one controllable process goal tied to a value, and judge yourself on that — whatever the scoreboard says.

The old agenda

Relax to get rid of the nerves — “calm down,” lower the arousal, make the butterflies go away. The message: this feeling is bad and must be removed.

The ACT-consistent way

You’re not deleting arousal — you’re making room for it and steadying attention. Breathing is your anchor (Connect), not a switch to force calm. Reframe nerves as energy and readiness (workability): the butterflies can fly in formation.

Try this

When nerves spike, name them, then use one long slow exhale to ground — not to erase. Let the energy be there and aim it at the task.

The old agenda

Block it out. Don’t think about the crowd, the score, the mistake. Build a wall.

The ACT-consistent way

Suppression backfires (Module 01) — walls fall. Instead: notice your attention has drifted, unhook from the distraction, and gently return to your anchor or task cue. Flexible attention, not a wall you have to hold up.

Try this

Expect to get distracted. Your skill isn’t never drifting — it’s the return. Every time you come back to the next job, that’s one rep.

The old agenda

You need to feel confident to perform — so build the belief first, then act. Which means on the days the feeling doesn’t come, you’re stuck.

The ACT-consistent way

Confidence follows committed action — it doesn’t have to come first. You can act on your values with courage while the doubt is still there. Redefine confidence as trust that you can handle whatever shows up — which is just psychological flexibility by another name.

Try this

Stop waiting to feel ready. Take the values-based action with the doubt on board; the confidence catches up behind it.

The old agenda

Forget it, move on, don’t dwell — which usually means suppress it and hope it doesn’t leak into the next play.

The ACT-consistent way

A three-second ACE reset: Acknowledge the frustration (it’s allowed), drop anchor, re-engage with the next play. Recovery, not erasure — you carry the feeling and keep playing.

Try this

Build a reset cue — one breath and one word (“next”). When you err: feel it, feet, next job.

03 · Putting it together

One big moment,
every tool at once

Here’s how the tools stack into a single, seamless approach to a high-pressure moment — a final, a big lift, an exam, a performance. Notice every tool is pointed at the same thing: being open, present and values-driven.

The 24 hours before → the moment itself
Your ACT-consistent game plan
1
The night before · imageryRun 2 minutes of coping imagery: picture the nerves and a rocky start, and see yourself drop anchor and execute your process. You’re rehearsing the choice point, not a fantasy.
2
Warm-up · goalsSet one process goal tied to a value (“compete with courage on every ball”). That’s what you’ll judge yourself on — not the result.
3
On the line · your routineRun your ACE routine. A — name the nerves, make room. C — feet, one long exhale (breathing as an anchor, not a sedative), eyes to your visual anchor. E — process cue, first job.
4
Mid-performance · self-talk & focusWhen the mind gets loud, defuse (“thanks, mind”) and use your process cue. When attention drifts, notice and return — the return is the skill, not never drifting.
5
After a mistake · the resetFeel it, feet, next job. A three-second ACE reset — recovery, not erasure.
6
Afterwards · reviewJudge the day on your values and process, not just the outcome. What did you stand for? Where did you run ACE well? That’s the scoreboard that builds a career — and a life.

Same tools you may have used for years — now every one of them serves openness, presence and your values, instead of a losing war with your own head.

06 · The most important part

You are the sky,
not the sport

This is the part that matters most, and it’s why THRIVE was always a life program. Sport can become so much of who you are that when it wobbles — injury, deselection, a bad run, retirement — your whole identity wobbles with it. Psychological flexibility is your protection: you are the observer, the sky — not any single storm that passes through, and not your results.

So invest in a life that’s bigger than your sport. Keep your values alive across all your domains, not just the field. Then, even if you stopped competing tomorrow, you’d keep every one of these tools — for study, work, relationships, and every hard and beautiful thing your twenties will throw at you.

07 · Maintenance

Keep it alive

A skill you don’t use quietly fades. Here’s a light maintenance kit — pick the bits that fit your life and make them routine.

  • Weekly

    A 60-second check-in: rate Be Open / Be Present / Do What Matters out of 10, and give a rep this week to whichever is lowest.

  • Daily

    Keep one anchor practice — a minute of feet-and-breath, or your ACE routine before something that matters.

  • Seasonal

    Re-take the values card-sort each season — you grow and change; your compass should keep up.

  • Plan ahead

    Write if-then ACE plans for your top three pressure moments (“if I miss, then feel it, feet, next job”).

  • Be kind

    Self-compassion on the bad days: talk to yourself the way you’d talk to a teammate who’s struggling. It’s not soft — it’s what keeps you in the game.

  • Reference

    Revisit any module anytime. THRIVE is a reference you keep, not a course you finish.

Small and specific. What’s the one practice you’ll actually keep?

You’ve completed THRIVE

Eight modules. Three moves. One skill set for every arena of your life. You can be open, come back to the present, and do what matters — even when your head and body throw everything at you. That’s not a certificate; it’s a capacity you now own. Kia kaha.

This isn’t goodbye. Screenshot your ACE Toolkit, keep your one commitment, and come back to any module whenever life gets loud. If you’re ever struggling beyond what a self-guided program can hold, that’s not weakness — reach out. The support-line button is on every page.
