USING IMAGERY
WELL

Rehearse the moments that matter, not the whole game. Imagery is practice you do in your head, using your senses, your body and your words. This page takes you from picking the right moment to a script you can run in twenty seconds on the day.

Pick the moment,
not the movie.

Imagery means running a skill or a moment in your mind, with as much of the real thing as you can bring: what you see, hear and feel, the pressure, and the words you say to yourself.

Most athletes who try it sit down and attempt to picture the whole game or race. It usually goes one of two ways. Everything goes perfectly, like a highlight reel, or the mind drifts off somewhere around the second quarter. Neither trains much. Good imagery is short, specific and honest. One moment that matters, run the way it really happens, with a plan for what you do when the doubt turns up.

Does it actually work?

A large review of imagery studies in sport found a medium sized benefit on average. It helped performance, and it also helped motivation and how athletes felt. Imagery added to physical practice did better than physical practice alone, and more intensive imagery training tended to do better than lighter versions.

So it earns its place, but as part of your training, not instead of it. And how you do it matters. That is what the seven steps below are for.

Is it a moment you dread? If just thinking about it brings up a lot of fear, start in the Rehearsal Room on the Open page. That tool is built for taking the charge out of a dreaded moment. Once it has come down, come back here and rehearse how you want to perform it.

Worked example · watch first

One moment.
One picture.

Before the how, here is what imagery can look like inside a real athlete’s head. This short animated film from The New York Times follows US biathlete Lowell Bailey.

Biathlon mixes cross country skiing with rifle shooting. You race hard on skis, then have to settle enough to hit a small target. It is a sport where the pressure builds inside the race itself, which makes it a good place to see imagery doing its job.

What to watch for

The film’s own summary describes it simply. As the pressure builds, Bailey wonders, “Can I do this?” Then he looks at the snow ahead and imagines himself as a bow with an arrow drawn, ready to accelerate. In those few seconds you can see most of this page at work.

  1. A moment, not the whole race. The imagery is tied to one point: when the pressure starts to build. That is an anchor moment. Step 01
  2. The doubt shows up, and stays in the picture. “Can I do this?” Nobody edits the thought out or argues with it. It is part of the real moment, so it is part of the imagery. That is the Acknowledge in ACE. Step 03
  3. He looks at the snow ahead. His attention goes outside, to something real and right in front of him, in the direction he is going. That is Connect, and it points him at the job rather than at the worry. Step 03
  4. He becomes a bow with an arrow drawn. One picture that holds the posture, the stored energy and the intent to go. That is a cue. It does the same job as cue words, just with an image instead. That is Engage. Step 04
Why it is a good example

Look at how little there is. One moment, one honest thought, one place to look, one picture. It doesn’t promise him a result and it doesn’t pretend the pressure isn’t there. It gives him something to do with it.

We’ve built this breakdown from the film’s own description. Watch it yourself and notice what else you pick up: what he sees, what he feels, and how fast it all happens.

01

Pick Your Anchor Moments

Stop trying to picture the whole performance. Pick the few moments that set everything else up.

What an anchor moment is

An anchor moment is a short, important point that comes round every time you compete. You can rehearse it properly, at real speed, in detail. Get these right and the rest of the performance has something to hang off. They come in three kinds.

Kind 1

Starts

Your first action. The gun, the whistle, the first touch, the first serve, the first tee shot. A clear first action gives everything after it a place to start from.

Kind 2

Big moments

The kick at goal, the putt, the penalty, the last 200 metres. The points where the pressure piles up and the outcome feels close.

Kind 3

After a mistake

Your first action after an error. Almost nobody rehearses this one, and it often decides what the next five minutes look like.

Size it to your sport

How much you script depends on how long your event is. A 100 metre sprint and a marathon need very different plans.

Under a minute

The whole event

A 100 metre sprint, a vault, a single run. It is short enough to rehearse from start to finish at real speed.

A few minutes

The start plus 2 to 3 key points

A 1500 metre race, a floor routine, a heat in the pool. Script the start, then the two or three points that decide it.

A full game or a long event

2 to 3 anchors

Rugby, football, netball, a marathon. Pick two or three anchors and give each its own short script.

02

Make It Real

The closer your imagery is to the real thing, the more of it carries over.

PETTLEP: a checklist for real

Imagery works best when it shares as much as possible with the actual performance. Researchers Paul Holmes and Dave Collins turned that idea into a seven part checklist called PETTLEP. You don’t need every letter every time. Use it to spot what your imagery is missing.

P

Physical

Stand in your stance, wear your kit, hold the ball, club or stick. Let your body join in.

E

Environment

Picture the actual venue. If you can, go there first, or look at photos or footage of it.

T

Task

The real skill at your level, with the thoughts and focus you would actually have. Not someone else’s highlight reel.

T

Timing

Real speed, most of the time. Slow it down only to learn a part, then bring it back up to speed.

L

Learning

Update the script as your skill and your role change. A script that never changes goes stale.

E

Emotion

Bring in the nerves, the noise and what is at stake. Calm and perfect isn’t what you will meet on the day.

P

Perspective

Through your own eyes, or like a camera watching you. Try both. Many athletes find their own eyes best for timing and feel, and the camera view useful for checking shape and position.

Then go beyond pictures

“Visualisation” makes it sound like a film, but the strongest imagery uses every sense. What can you see, hear, feel in your body, touch, even smell? And what do you feel emotionally?

Some athletes hardly “see” anything when they do imagery, and that is fine. Lead with the sense that comes most easily, often the feel of the movement or the sound of the venue, and add the others one at a time.

03

Let the Noise In

Don’t script the perfect day. Script the real one, and practise what you do when the doubt shows up.

Why a perfect script lets you down

Most imagery scripts are spotless. Nothing goes wrong and nobody is nervous. Then the real day arrives with sweaty palms and a loud thought about last time, and the script has nothing to say about it.

Mental noise is normal. Doubt, nerves and old memories turn up for good athletes too. The aim isn’t to get rid of them. The aim is to have practised what you do when they arrive. So write them in.

Don’t write “I feel no nerves” or “don’t miss”. Trying hard not to think about something can make it more likely, especially under pressure. In one study, people putting a golf ball who were told not to overshoot it were more likely to overshoot when their minds were busy with something else. Give your mind something to do instead.

ACE inside the moment

This is the same ACE you learned in Dropping Anchor, shrunk down to a few seconds and placed inside your anchor moment.

  1. A, Acknowledge. Name what is there, briefly and without arguing. “There’s the worry.” “There’s that memory.”
  2. C, Connect. Come back to your body and where you are. Feet into the floor, one breath out, eyes on something real.
  3. E, Engage. Back to the job, with your cue. “High and soft.” “Next job.”
In a script it sounds like this

From Mia’s script in Step 05. Mia is a fictional basketballer.

“A thought shows up: I missed this exact shot in the first half. There’s that memory. I press my feet into the floor and let one slow breath out.”

The thought is there. It gets named in three words. Then her attention goes to her feet and her breath, and from there to the job.

04

Choose Your Words

A few words that tell your body what to do, at the exact point it needs telling.

Words that work

Cue words are the short phrases you say to yourself inside the moment. Good ones are simple and point you somewhere. Unhelpful ones are usually warnings, lectures or insults.

  1. Say what to do. Not what to avoid. Your body needs a direction, not a ban.
  2. Point at the target or the result. “Through the ball” usually works better than a list of body parts. Focusing on the effect you want, out in the world, tends to keep the movement smooth.
  3. Fit the job. Fine, precise skills like a putt, a shot or a kick suit technical cues. Effort and endurance moments suit drive cues like “Go now” or “Strong”.
  4. Stay short and yours. One to three words, in language you would actually use. A picture can work too, like Bailey’s bow and arrow.

05

Write Your Script

Two versions of the same moment: a full one that builds the picture, and a short one you can run anywhere.

Two versions

Full script · 250 to 350 words

Builds the picture

First person, present tense, every sense, the noise and your ACE. Read it slowly once a session, or record it in your own voice and listen.

Short version · 40 to 80 words

The one you use

The same moment at game speed, cut down to the key beats. Run it 10 to 20 times a session, and in the warm up on the day.

How to build it
  1. Set the scene. Where you are, the score or the stakes, what is about to happen.
  2. Bring in the senses. Use your notes from Step 02. What you see, hear, feel and touch.
  3. Let the noise in. The thought or feeling that turns up, and your ACE from Step 03.
  4. The action, with your cues. Place each cue at the point the movement needs it, from Step 04.
  5. Finish with the next job. Whatever happened, what you do next. Don’t script the result. Script what you do.
Worked example · Mia, a fictional basketballer

Mia is made up, and this is how an athlete might write their own full script. The labels show where her cues and ACE steps sit. She leaves them out when she reads it.

Acknowledge Connect Engage Cue Reset

Mia’s full script · about 250 words

Fourth quarter, we’re down by two with forty seconds left. I jog to the left corner and set my toes just behind the line. The gym is loud. Shoes squeak, the bench is on its feet and someone in the crowd is yelling. The lights bounce off the floor. My palms are sweaty, so I wipe them on my shorts, and I can feel my heart thumping in my chest.

A thought shows up: I missed this exact shot in the first half. Acknowledge There’s that memory. Connect I press my feet into the floor and let one slow breath out. I’m here to be the teammate who’s ready when the ball comes, whatever the score.

Our guard drives baseline and I stay on the balls of my feet, eyes on her. Her eyes find me. Cue ‘Ready hands.’ I show her a target, low and wide. The pass pops into my pocket and my fingers find the seams. My knees are already bending.

Another thought: don’t miss. Acknowledge There’s the worry. It can come too. Engage ‘High and soft.’ My legs push up through the floor, my elbow stays under the ball and my wrist snaps through. I feel the ball roll off my fingertips. Cue ‘Hold it.’ My follow through stays high, fingers pointing at the rim, and I watch the ball arc up towards the back of the ring.

Reset ‘Next job.’ Whatever the scoreboard says, I sprint back on defence, find my number and call the switch. Hands up, talking, ready for the next play.

Mia’s short version · about 50 words

Left corner, toes behind the line. Loud gym, sweaty palms, heart thumping. There’s that memory, there’s the worry. Feet into the floor, one breath out. Ready hands. Catch in my pocket, fingers on the seams. High and soft. Legs, elbow, wrist. Hold it. Next job, sprint back and find my number.

Notice what Mia never imagines: the ball going in. The script stops at the ball in the air and moves straight to her next job. She is rehearsing what she can control, so the script still works on the nights the shot misses.

06

Practise and Build

Little and often. A script you run three times a week beats a perfect one in a drawer.

How much
3
Sessions a week
Spread across the week, not crammed.
10–15
Minutes a session
Long enough for real reps.
10–20
Short version reps
After one read of the full script.

This lines up with the research. A review of 133 imagery training studies found people practised, on average, about three times a week for around 17 minutes a session. In a basketball study, 13 and 18 minute sessions improved free throws more than 8 minute sessions did. The shorter sessions still helped compared with no imagery at all. They just helped less.

Build it in layers
  1. Weeks 1 and 2: make it clear. A quiet spot, your stance, your kit. Add one sense at a time until the moment feels real and you can run it the way you mean to.
  2. From week 3: add pressure. Run it with crowd noise playing, straight after a hard set while you are still breathing hard, or right before a real rep at training. Make the noise in your script louder.
  3. Then: use it on the day. The short version in your warm up, and in the seconds before your anchor moment.
Where to fit it

Attach it to training rather than adding another job to your day. Straight after a session, while the feel of the skill is fresh, is a good slot. So is the five minutes before training starts. The short version fits anywhere: the bus, the queue, the walk to the car.

Same idea as the Practice Room: build it in, don’t fit it in.

07

Review and Refine

Your script is a draft. Real performances tell you what to change.

After you compete

Within a day or two, while it is fresh, look back at your anchor moment. Not at the result. At what happened in the moment itself.

  1. Did the moment go the way you rehearsed it? Where did it match, and where did it surprise you?
  2. Did your cue come when you needed it? On time, late, or not at all?
  3. What showed up that wasn’t in your script? A thought, a sound, a situation you hadn’t pictured.

Then change one thing in the script. That is the L in PETTLEP: the script learns as you do. One change at a time keeps it yours and keeps it short.

When imagery goes wrong

Very common, and it isn’t a sign of anything bad. Don’t fight it. Acknowledge it like any other noise, then rewind a few seconds and run that part again, slowly first, then at real speed.

If it keeps happening and comes with a lot of dread, the moment may need some work in the Rehearsal Room first. Bring it to a session too.

Plenty of athletes don’t get clear pictures, and they can still use imagery well. Lead with the sense that is strongest for you, often the feel of the movement or the sound of the venue.

Then add one layer at a time. Photos or footage of the venue help with the visual side, and so does doing your imagery straight after a real rep.

Slow is useful for learning one part of a skill. Most of your reps should still be at real speed, because that is the speed you will perform at.

A simple check: time the real movement, then time your imagery of it. If the imagery takes twice as long, speed it up.

Some nerves are the point. Your script should feel a bit like the real thing, and the real thing comes with nerves. Use your Connect move and keep going.

If it climbs a long way, shorten the session, keep your eyes open, and go back to the clarity layer before adding pressure again. If it stays high, talk to us about it.

Lying in bed is a great place to sleep and a poor place for imagery. Do it standing or sitting up, in your stance if you can, with your gear in your hands. That is the Physical in PETTLEP doing its job.

And when your mind drifts, notice it and come back. Like the Practice Room says, the returning is the rep.

Using imagery well in seven lines

  • Pick the moment, not the movie. Starts, big moments and your first action after a mistake.
  • Make it real. Your stance, your venue, real speed, every sense.
  • Let the noise in. Script the doubt, then practise ACE inside the moment.
  • Say what to do. Short cues that point at the target, not warnings.
  • Two versions. A full script to build it, a short one to use it.
  • Three times a week, 10 to 15 minutes. Clear first, then add pressure.
  • Script what you do, not the result. Then let each real performance improve the script.
