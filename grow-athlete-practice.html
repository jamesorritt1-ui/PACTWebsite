Don’t fit it in.
Build it in.

The single most common reason athletes stop doing this is that they treated it as one more item on a list that is already too long.

So don’t add it. Attach it to something you already do. The walk from the car park. The queue at the physio. The two minutes between sets. The first thirty seconds of your warm-up. You are not looking for spare time — you don’t have any — you are looking for moments that already exist and are currently being spent on your phone.

What you’re actually training

Not relaxation. Relaxation is a possible side effect, not the purpose — and if you make it the purpose, you’ve turned the practice into another struggle strategy.

What you’re training is the ability to put your attention where you choose and bring it back when it wanders. Every time you notice your mind has gone and you return it, that’s the rep. Not the staying — the returning. An athlete whose mind wandered forty times and came back forty times did forty reps. Someone who sat there placidly did none.

That reframe matters, because it means there is no such thing as being bad at this. A busy mind isn’t a failed practice; it’s a heavier set.

It can feel worse before it feels better. When you stop moving and start paying attention, things that were in the background come forward. Think of a lake: the water looks opaque until the silt settles, and then you can see what’s on the bottom — including the odd wrecked car. Those things were always there. You’re just seeing them now. That’s normal, it settles, and if it doesn’t, that’s exactly what your sessions with us are for.

01

The 9-Second Check

Three seconds each on mind, body and breath. Short enough that “no time” stops being an argument.

The practice
  1. Three seconds — mind. What thoughts are here? What emotions? Don’t analyse them. Just clock what’s running.
  2. Three seconds — body. Sweep head to toe. What’s normal, what’s different, what’s sore, what’s tight, what’s loose?
  3. Three seconds — breath. Where is it, how fast, how deep? See if you can notice it without changing it — harder than it sounds, and the noticing is the skill.

That’s the whole thing. Nine seconds gives you a genuine read on your own status. If it’s good, carry on. If it isn’t, you now know early enough to do something.

Why so short

Because a practice you’ll actually do beats a better practice you won’t. Nine seconds fits in a substitution, a drinks break, the walk back to your mark, the lift up to the start. There is no version of your week that doesn’t have room for it.

And it gets you the thing that matters most: early information. Most athletes only notice they’ve gone when they’re a long way gone.

02

CPR Under Pressure

The moment the game is on you and your body starts tightening up.

The practice

Three letters, in order, in about thirty seconds.

  1. C — Check in. What’s in your body, and where has your mind gone? Usually it has gone to what happens if I miss — which is the future, not the shot.
  2. P — Pause with a breath. One or two, and actually notice them flowing. This is the bit that interrupts the tightening.
  3. R — Reset, then refocus. Reset means restart your routine from the top — the batter stepping out and back in, the server bouncing the ball again. Refocus means pointing at what you want to happen, not what you’re trying to avoid.

Point at the thing, not away from it. “Don’t hit it long” puts long in your head, and your body is remarkably obliging. “Deep and left” gives it somewhere to go. This one small habit is worth more than most of what gets called mental toughness.

Feel the difference yourself

Take whatever you hold — bat, racquet, club, stick, or just your hands — and make your normal movement with every muscle deliberately tight. Notice what happens to the motion, to your breathing, and to your head.

Now run C, P, R and make the identical movement with the tension out. Most athletes report the second version feels not just easier but more accurate, and that their attention had somewhere to go. That difference is what you’re protecting when you use this.

03

The 2BSET Sweep

A full read of your whole system in about two minutes, in a fixed order so you can’t skip the awkward bits.

The practice

Five channels, always in the same sequence. The order is deliberate: it starts with the easiest thing to find and finishes with the hardest, so you arrive at your thoughts already steady rather than diving straight into the noise.

  • B — Breath. Just notice it. Don’t fix it.
  • B — Body. Feet on the ground, hands, the feel of clothing on skin, the area around your eyes.
  • S — Senses. One thing you can see, hear, smell, feel, taste. Noticing an absence counts.
  • E — Emotions. Name what’s here. Then watch the intensity change on its own, without you doing anything to it.
  • T — Thoughts. Watch them go past like clouds. You are not required to board any of them.
Why the order

Because most athletes, left to themselves, go straight to T — and thoughts are the loudest, stickiest, least reliable channel of the five. Starting at the breath and working outwards means that by the time you get to thinking, you have four other sources of information already in view, and the commentary has to compete rather than dominate.

There’s also a useful move at the end: find where an emotion sits in your body, then breathe into that spot. It won’t remove the feeling. It tends to take the edge off it, and more importantly it keeps you in contact with it rather than in flight from it.

04

Your Sense Map

Working out which of your five senses gets you back to now the fastest.

The practice

Your senses only ever operate in the present tense. You cannot smell last Tuesday or hear next week. That makes them the most direct route back into the moment there is — more reliable than any instruction to “focus”.

But people aren’t equal across the five, and almost nobody knows which of theirs is strongest. Once you do, you have a shortcut: when you need to be here right now, you go to your strongest sense first rather than fishing.

The point of writing it down

Filling this in forces you to notice detail you have been playing alongside for years without registering: the specific squeak of your shoes on that surface, the smell of the changing room, the taste of your mouthguard, the exact sound the ball makes off the middle.

That inventory becomes a resource. When you’re lost in your own head at 3–1 down, “find the sound of feet on the court” is a real instruction you can follow. “Concentrate” is not.

05

Visual Anchors

Fixed points in your venue that you can put your eyes on when your head goes.

The practice

Pick physical objects you can always find where you play, and use them deliberately. A flag. The line markings. A floodlight. The scoreboard. A sticker on your bat. A wristband you can flick. Your own laces.

When your attention is spiralling, you put your eyes on the anchor and take a few slow breaths while you look at it — noticing its actual detail: shape, texture, colour, whether it’s moving.

Why it works

Because it’s far easier to move attention than to stop it. Telling yourself to stop thinking about the missed shot doesn’t work — you already know this. Giving your eyes a specific, external, physically present job does, because attention has somewhere to go.

It also travels. You can’t always take your routine with you, but there is always a line, a post, a light or a sleeve.

06

Breathing Through It

The moments you stop breathing properly and don’t notice for another twenty seconds.

The practice

Under stress, athletes hold their breath or breathe shallow and high in the chest. It happens automatically and it happens at the worst possible time — while your muscles are tightening and you’re trying to execute something skilful.

You breathe roughly twenty thousand times a day without deciding to. This is about deliberately taking hold of a handful of those, at the moments that count.

  1. Catch it. Notice you’ve stopped breathing normally — that’s the whole skill and it’s the bit that takes practice.
  2. Longer out than in. Breathe in for a comfortable count, out for a bit longer. Don’t force the volume; extend the exhale.
  3. Low, not high. Let the movement be in your belly rather than your shoulders. If your shoulders are lifting, you’re breathing high.
  4. Three of them, then back to the job. Not ten. Three, and then your attention goes back out to the task.

Keep it gentle. Deliberately deep or forceful breathing can make you light-headed or tingly and will make anxiety worse, not better. Slow and small beats big. If you feel dizzy, stop and breathe normally — and use a visual anchor instead, which does the same job with none of the physiology.

07

The Sitting Body Scan

The foundation practice. Everything else gets easier once this is in place.

The practice

Move your attention slowly through your body, area by area, noticing what’s there without trying to change any of it. Six minutes, sitting, eyes closed or softly lowered.

Two things you are not doing: relaxing, and fixing. If tension shows up, you notice the tension. If nothing shows up in an area, you notice the nothing. That’s a legitimate result and it’s data.

Why athletes get more from this than most people

Because you already have an unusually detailed relationship with your body — you just use it for a narrow purpose. You can tell whether a hamstring is at 80% or 90%. You can feel a technical fault in a movement. That’s a real skill and most people don’t have it.

What this practice does is widen that same attention from performance monitoring to everything else: the tension you carry the night before, where dread actually sits, what your body does in the twenty minutes after a loss. That information has always been there. You’ve simply never been asked to read it for anything other than output.

08

The Mindful Warm-Up

Turning something you already do every session into the practice, so it costs you nothing.

The practice

You already warm up. You already stretch, jog, run lines, take shots. Most athletes do all of it on autopilot with their mind somewhere else entirely — which is a wasted twenty minutes twice a day, five days a week.

Same warm-up. Attention actually in it.

  • Stretching: feel where the stretch actually is, and notice the exact point where it changes from lengthening to strain. Notice the difference between your two sides.
  • Walking or jogging the lines: feel each foot land and lift. Match your breathing to your stride. When your mind goes — and it will — bring it back to the feet.
  • First touches: the very first ten contacts with the ball, given full attention. Weight, texture, sound, where it meets your hand or foot.
Why this is the highest-value practice on the page

Because it requires no extra time whatsoever, and because it trains attention in the exact environment where you need it. Sitting on a bedroom floor builds the general capacity. Doing it on your own pitch, in your own kit, with your own noise around you, builds the thing you will actually use on Saturday.

It also fixes something else. Athletes who warm up on autopilot arrive at the first whistle having not yet turned up. This has you present before the game starts rather than three minutes into it.

There’s an expectation hidden in that sentence — that a mind is supposed to be quiet. It isn’t. That’s just what minds are like, and some days more than others.

Noticing it has wandered and bringing it back is the entire exercise. If that happened thirty times, you did thirty reps. Nobody thinks a heavy set means they’re bad at lifting.

The paradox is that minds usually settle once you genuinely stop demanding that they do.

Could it be that you’re tired? Athletes carry a lot of sleep debt, and the moment you stop moving it shows up. The honest answer is usually more sleep — see the sleep tool.

In the meantime: sit up rather than lying down, open your eyes slightly, put more light in the room, slide forward off the back of the chair, or try it standing. Practise at a different time of day — straight after a hard session is a hard sell.

This is common early on and it has a mechanism. When you stop and pay attention, things that were in the background come forward. They were always there; you’re just seeing them now that the water has cleared.

Firefighters have a word for something similar — backdraft. Open a door on a closed, oxygen-starved fire and the fresh air makes it flare. Turning fresh attention onto a closed-off part of yourself can do the same.

What to do: stay with it if you can, dip a toe rather than diving, and back off and come back later if it’s too much. And bring it to a session — this is exactly the sort of thing that is much better worked through with someone than alone.

There’s evidence behind the distinction. When people were randomly assigned to mindfulness training, relaxation training, or nothing, both active groups improved mood — but only the mindfulness group showed reduced distraction and rumination. That is the mechanism you want on a pitch, and relaxation doesn’t deliver it.

The Practice Room in five lines

  • Build it in, don’t fit it in. Attach it to moments you already have.
  • The returning is the rep, not the staying. A wandering mind is a heavier set, not a failure.
  • Nine seconds counts. Consistency beats duration, especially at the start.
  • It is not relaxation. Calm is a side effect; attention is the point.
  • Worse before better is normal. The silt settles and you see what was always there.
