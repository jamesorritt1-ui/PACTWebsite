Be Open: Defusion

01 · The second move of Being Open

A thought is a thought —
not a command, not a fact

In Module 02 you learned acceptance: making room for difficult feelings. This module is the other half of Being Open, and it's about difficult thoughts. It's called defusion.

Here's the idea. All day long your mind produces words and pictures — a running commentary. Usually you don't notice the words as words; you're fused with them, looking through them at the world, treating them as simple truth. That's cognitive fusion. When you're fused with "I'm going to choke," it doesn't feel like a sentence — it feels like a fact about the future, and your body reacts as if it's already true.

Defusion is the skill of stepping back and seeing a thought for what it actually is: a string of words, a passing picture, a bit of mental weather — not a command you must obey or a truth you must believe. Seen clearly, "I'm not good enough" is a thought. That's all. It might show up a thousand times; it's still just a thought.

"You don't have to argue with your mind, and you don't have to believe it. You just have to notice it's talking — and keep doing what matters."

— The heart of defusion

This completes the A of ACE. To Acknowledge is to notice both the feelings (acceptance) and the thoughts (defusion) with a bit of curiosity — instead of being run by either. Crucially, defusion is not positive thinking. You're not trying to swap a bad thought for a nice one, or argue yourself into confidence. You're just changing your relationship to the thought so it has less grip. The words can stay exactly the same — they just stop running the show.

02 · The tell-tale difference

Hooked, or unhooked?

A simple way to feel the difference: when a thought shows up, are you hooked by it (fused) or unhooked from it (defused)? Same thought, completely different grip.

Hooked (fused)
  • The thought feels like the plain truth
  • You obey it, or fight hard to argue it away
  • It grabs all your attention
  • It steers your behaviour without you noticing
  • "I am going to choke."
Unhooked (defused)
  • You see the thought as a thought
  • You notice it without believing or obeying it
  • There's a bit of space around it
  • You choose your next move regardless
  • "I'm having the thought that I'll choke."

Read those last two lines again. "I am going to choke" and "I'm having the thought that I'll choke" contain almost the same words — but one is a verdict you're trapped inside, and the other is something you're simply noticing. That tiny shift is defusion in a nutshell, and it's trainable.

03 · See it move

Passengers on the bus

Here's the picture that makes defusion click. Imagine your life is a bus, and you're the driver, heading toward what matters to you. Over the years, passengers have climbed aboard — your difficult thoughts. Self-Doubt, the Comparison, Fear of Failure, the Critic. They're loud, they're rude, and they yell directions from the back.

Two things are true about these passengers. First, you can't throw them off — the more you try, the more you have to stop the bus and wrestle them, and the bus goes nowhere. Second — and this is everything — they can shout, but they can't touch the wheel. Only you drive. Try it: each passenger will pipe up. See what happens when you obey them, versus when you let them shout and keep driving toward what matters.

Journey toward what matters

Did you notice? Obeying or arguing stops the bus — you hand a passenger the wheel. Thanking it and driving on keeps you moving with the noise still aboard. You never had to win the argument or empty the bus. You just had to keep both hands on the wheel. That's defusion in action.

04 · Your toolkit

The Defuser

Defusion is a set of small, trainable moves. Type in a thought that genuinely hooks you — something your mind says that stings, like "I'm not good enough" — then tap each technique to feel how it loosens the grip. The words barely change; your relationship to them does.

None of these argue with the thought or try to prove it wrong. They just create a sliver of space — and in that space, you get to choose the next move instead of the thought choosing for you. The one that fits will be different for everyone; keep the two or three that work best for you.

Apply in Life

Defusion is gold for social comparison (especially scrolling), imposter feelings at uni or a first job, and harsh self-talk. Catch one brutal self-statement this week and unhook from it — it's the exact skill your twenties ask for.

06 · Big ideas to carry

The general ideas
worth keeping

If you take nothing else from this module, take these. They apply on the pitch, in the exam hall, in your headphones at 2am — anywhere your mind gets loud.

Six things to remember

  • A thought is a thought — not a command to obey, and not a fact to believe.
  • You don't have to win the argument with your mind. You just don't have to hand it the wheel.
  • Defusion is not positive thinking. You're not making thoughts nicer — just holding them more lightly.
  • The passengers usually don't leave — and they don't need to. You can drive with them aboard.
  • Feel the gap between "I'm not good enough" and "I'm having the thought that I'm not good enough."
  • Unhooking is a rep, not a one-off. Practise on small thoughts, and it's there for the big ones.

Your defusion pocket card

  1. Catch it — notice you're hooked (tight, narrow, reacting).
  2. Name it — "I'm having the thought that…"
  3. Thank it — "Thanks, mind." No argument needed.
  4. Keep driving — take the next values-based action anyway.

07 · This week's Mind Gym

Practise it for real

Defusion only becomes yours through reps in your actual life. Here's a full week of it — first, name your regular passengers, then pick a couple of experiments to run, and log a line a day so you can watch the grip loosen.

Step 1 · Name your passengers

Giving a recurring thought a name is itself a defusion move — it turns "the truth" into "that character again". Add the ones that ride with you most. (Saved on this device.)

No passengers named yet — add the thoughts that show up most.

Step 2 · Experiments to try this week

You don't need to do all of these — pick two or three that fit your week and actually run them.

  • On the pitch

    Before a session, pick your loudest passenger. When it shouts mid-training, silently say "Thanks, mind" and run your next play. Notice you can perform with it still yelling.

  • Everyday

    Once a day, catch a harsh self-statement and re-say it as "I'm having the thought that…". Feel the half-step of space it opens up.

  • Phone

    Next time you compare yourself while scrolling, name it out loud in your head: "Ah — here's the Comparison passenger." Then choose: put the phone down, or keep scrolling on your terms.

  • Silly voice

    Take your stickiest thought and say it in a cartoon character's voice, or sing it to "Happy Birthday". Do it until it's just sound. This proves it's only words.

  • Name the story

    Give your recurring worry a title — "the Not-Good-Enough story". When it shows up, just note: "ah, that story again" — and carry on.

  • Diary

    Keep the 3-line passenger log below — one entry a day. Patterns show up fast, and so does progress.

Step 3 · The 7-day passenger log

One entry a day for seven days. Tap a day, jot the loudest passenger that showed up, the defusion move you used, and whether unhooking helped. No judgement — honesty is the training. Everything saves on this device only.

0 / 7 days

Lock it in

That's the whole of Being Open. Acceptance handles the feelings; defusion handles the thoughts. Together they are the A of ACE — Acknowledge: noticing whatever shows up, without being run by it. Next, we move to the C — Connect: coming back into your body and the present moment, so you've got a steady place to drive from.
